screening tools: Psychometrics and considerations for future use

Background: Developmental screening improves the detection of developmental concerns, yet numerous children
are  not  screened/assessed.  Remote  child  developmental  tool  administration  has  been  utilized  to  increase
screening and assessment accessibility.
Method: We conducted a realist review to: (1) identify existing multi-domain child development assessment and
screening  tools  for  children  0–5  years;  (2)  review  psychometric  data  on  their  digital  (i.e.,  only  administered
remotely) administration; and (3) explore contextual factors relevant to their digital administration. We searched
APA PsycInfo, MEDLINE, CINAHL, and ERIC to identify tools and papers on their psychometrics. We reference-
searched included articles and searched Google for relevant grey literature.
Results: Of 33 multi-domain child development tools identified in objective one, five tools (in five studies) were
delivered  digitally  and  compared  to  traditional  (e.g.,  paper)  delivery  (i.e.,  objective  two).  Studies  evaluated
within-group equivalence reliability (k = 2) and between-group equivalence (k = 3). Within-group equivalence
reliability was established for the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, and domains (e.g., gross motor) of the Ages
and Stages Questionnaires 2nd edition (ASQ-2) and Revised  Prescreening Denver  Questionnaire (R-PDQ). Be-
tween  group  equivalence  was  demonstrated  for  Developmental  Neuropsychological  Assessment,  2nd  Edition
(NEPSY-II)  subtests  and  Bayley  Scales  of  Infant  and  Toddler  Development,  3rd  edition  (Bayley-3)  items.  In
another between group evaluation, web-based and paper versions of the ASQ-2 were deemed generally equiv-
alent. Digital Bayley-3 inter-observer reliability ranged from 0.82 to 1.0. Examiner support, time, tool modifi-
cations, family resources, and comfort promotion supported digital administration.
Conclusion: Digitally delivered ASQ-2, R-PDQ, Vineland, and Bayley-3 and NEPSY-II components show promise
for equivalence with traditional administration.

1. Background

Developmental delay is a term used to describe children who struggle
with attaining developmental milestones, such as language and motor
skills [1]. Language difficulties in children are associated with current
and later internalizing (e.g., depression) and externalizing (e.g., hyper-
activity)  problems  [2].  Preschool-aged  children  with  cognitive  delays
are  significantly  more  likely  than  their  peers  to  exhibit  behavioral
concerns at age five [3] and early motor skills have been associated with
current  and  later  problem-solving  skills  [4].  However,  early  in-
terventions have been shown to improve children’s development [5,6].

Developmental  screening  and  evaluations  are  used  to  identify
developmental concerns and guide interventions to promote children’s
development. Developmental screening thus requires valid and reliable
tools  to  identify  children  at  risk  for  developmental  concerns  [7].
Developmental  screening  tools  are  administered  to  assess  children’s
developmental milestones to discover the possibility of a developmental
concern  and  determine  if  additional  resources  and  evaluations  are
needed  [8,9].  Since  they  are  not  diagnostic,  more  comprehensive,
formal, and time-consuming developmental evaluations (also referred to
as  developmental  assessments)  are  administered  by  trained  pro-
fessionals (e.g., pediatrician) when a concern has been identified, which

allows  for  the  identification  of  developmental  delays  [8,9].  Develop-

developmental delays and increase intervention referrals [10,11].

Unfortunately,  numerous  children  are  at  risk  for  developmental
concerns, and many do not benefit from screening. For example, 25 % of
children were suspected to have a developmental delay in a sample of
330,613 children from 63 countries [12]. Yet, in a sample of 9 million
young children in the United States, only 30 % received parent-reported
developmental screening in the previous year [13]; this is problematic as
developmental concerns could go undetected without screening. Chil-
dren of single mothers, those living in low socioeconomic status (SES)
homes, racial minorities, and families that did not speak English as their
primary language were less likely to receive screening than others [13].
Low  developmental  screening  rates  among  children  experiencing
vulnerability (e.g., low SES) are particularly problematic as these chil-
dren  are  often  at  greater  risk  for  developmental  concerns  than  their
peers (e.g., [14]). For example, overall developmental delay risk prev-
alence for children at four years of age in the Netherlands was more than
twice as high for children in low SES (preterm birth = 12.5 %, term birth
= 7.2 %) compared to high SES (preterm birth = 5.6 %, term birth = 2.8
%) homes [14]. Therefore, strategies are needed to promote equitable
access to developmental screening. Digitally delivered (i.e., remotely)
developmental screening initiatives have been offered to improve reach
for  diverse  groups,  such  as  families  with  low  incomes  in  the  United
States [15], families in Nairobi [16], and children requiring screening
for autism spectrum disorder [17].

1.1. Digitally delivered developmental screening and assessments

Before  the  coronavirus  19  (COVID-19)  pandemic,  research  was
emerging  on  remote  digital  developmental  screening.  For  example,
parents  completed  Ages  and  Stages,  3rd  edition  (ASQ-3;  [18])  forms
online  for  telehealth  assessments  [19],  parenting  programs  [20],  and
after their child was discharged from a pediatric intensive care unit [21].
In a pilot study of mothers with low incomes, children’s development
was screened with a text message-based tool; 98 % of mothers reported
that they would recommend the tool to a friend and 100 % of mothers
wished to partake in developmental screening in the future [15], which
is  promising  for  increased  screening  reach  for  families  experiencing
vulnerability related to low income.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, communities experienced a myriad
of disruptions to clinical services. Research was also disrupted by public
health  mandates.  These  disruptions  motivated  rapid  transitions  to
administering remote digitally delivered child development tools, such
as ASQ-3 screening (e.g., [22,23]), the Developmental Profile, 3rd Edi-
tion [24] and Developmental Assessment of Young Children 2nd Edition
(DAYC-2; [25]) [26,27]. In one study, the authors indicated that remote
digital evaluation outcomes were similar to in-clinic visits, but statistical
comparisons were not provided [28]. Given increases in remote digitally
delivered child developmental tools, a review on their psychometrics is
needed to guide best practices.

Review Objectives and Methods.
We conducted a realist review [29,30] to: (1) identify multi-domain
developmental  (i.e.,  evaluate  ≥ three  developmental  areas)  screening
and  evaluation  tools  for  children  aged  0–5  years;  (2)  review  psycho-
metric data on remote digitally delivered tools; and (3) review contex-
tual factors related to their remote digital delivery. To meet our aims, we
chose a realist review with a pragmatic approach of determining what
works, for whom, and under what conditions, instead of simply what
works [30,31].

Unlike systematic reviews, which frequently address precise research
questions (e.g., whether an intervention works), realist reviews have a
broad explanatory focus and are ideal for syntheses involving complex
social interventions [30], like digital child developmental tool admin-
istration. A common criticism of systematic reviews is the lack of focus
on context, yet context is central to realist reviews [32]. For example,

realist  reviews  seek  to  guide  program  development  by  synthesizing
contextual knowledge on how interventions can be employed to work
most  effectively  [30].  Additionally,  Pawson  argued  against  excluding
articles deemed methodologically weak (in contrast to practices in sys-
tematic reviews), as “nuggets of wisdom” can often be found in imper-
fect  studies  [33];  while  realist  reviews  support  the  inclusion  of  high
quality evidence, studies are included based on their relevance to the
research question. Realist reviews involve multiple, iterative searches,
which differ from more rigid, formulaic methods in systematic reviews
[30].

1.2. Search strategy and inclusion criteria

To  address  objective  one,  we  searched  APA  PsycInfo  to  identify
multi-domain child developmental tools with synonyms and truncated
versions  of  the  following  terms:  “early  childhood  development”  AND
“language”,  “cognitive”,  “mental”,  “motor”,  “communication”  AND
“screening  tool”  or  “psychometrics”.  Studies  were  limited  to  English
with children ≤ 5 years, which yielded 496 results. Two reviewers (JK
and lab research assistant) independently reviewed the tests and mea-
sures  section  to  identify  developmental  tools.  Tools  were  included  if
they assessed more than two developmental domains (e.g., communi-
cation, motor, social). Those that were no longer in use (i.e., unable to
locate publications in past 10 years or purchase/locate tool online) were
excluded.  90  %  agreement  was  obtained,  and  discrepancies  were
resolved with NL. Reference mining was utilized to identify other tools.
Findings  from  the  first  objective  informed  the  search  of  the  second
objective;  for  the  second  objective,  three  reviewers  (JK,  SK,  and  lab
research  assistant)  searched  APA  PsycInfo,  MEDLINE,  CINAHL,  and
ERIC with acquired tool names from objective one along with synonyms
of “psychometrics” (e.g., validity, reliability, reproducibility of results),
and  retrieved  4,110  articles.  Titles  and  abstracts  were  reviewed,  and
articles were included with psychometric data on preschool children’s
developmental tools administered by telephone interview (i.e., due to
potential use with cellphones), videoconferencing (e.g., telehealth, tel-
epractice) technology, or parental report via email, online, and smart-
phone  applications  (i.e.,  completed  remotely).  Publications  were
excluded  if  remote  digital  and  traditional  delivery  (e.g.,  in  person
observation, paper-pencil in-person or mail-based questionnaire) were
not differentiated. Authors JK and SK searched Google to obtain relevant
grey literature (i.e., publicly available articles that are not published in
peer  review  journals;  [34])  for  contextual  information  on  each  tool’s
delivery  (e.g.,  available  formats,  translations,  training  requirements,
included ages and domains) and also included peer reviewed papers that
provided  context  for  the  tool’s  use.  Reference  mining  was  utilized  to
identify relevant papers. To meet objective three, the included articles
from objective two were reviewed for contextual factors related to their
remote  digital  delivery.  Searches  commenced  in  May  2020  and  were
updated  in  December  2022–January  2023.  See  Fig.  1  for  the  search
strategy.

1.3. Analysis

For  objective  two,  JK  extracted  psychometric  data  from  each
included publication and SK confirmed each extraction. We evaluated
studies  for  equivalence  between  remote  digital  and  traditional  tool
administration, in addition to other forms of reliability (e.g., inter-rater)
and  validity  (e.g.,  concurrent).  Equivalence  reliability  and  mode
equivalence are terms utilized to assess the consistency of a tool when
administered in two or more versions [35,36]. In this review, we utilize
the term within-group equivalence reliability to classify studies that utilize
a single group to compare the remote digital delivery of a tool to another
version (e.g., paper) and between group equivalence to describe analyses
(i.e., remote digital delivery compared to another format) with two or
more groups. For the third objective, JK reviewed articles from objective
two for contextual considerations affecting digital remote delivery, and

2. Results

2.1. Study description

Our  search  identified  33  tools  utilized  to  assess  multiple  develop-
mental  domains  for  children  ≤ five  years  of  age  (objective  one).  Of
these,  we  found  five  peer-reviewed  publications  evaluating  the  psy-
chometrics of five of these tools when delivered remotely with digital
methods  (objective  two).  These  manuscripts  were  published  between
2006 and 2022, with studies conducted in Canada [37,38], United States
[39,40], and Italy [41]. Most studies focused on infants and toddlers,
with two publications including children greater than three years of age
[38,40].  Three  publications  employed  quasi-experimental  studies
[39–41] and two utilized correlational designs [37,38]. Screening tools
included the ASQ 2nd edition [42] and the Revised Prescreening Denver
Questionnaire  (R-PDQ;  [43]).  Assessment  tools  included  A  Develop-
mental  Neuropsychological  Assessment  2nd  Edition  (NEPSY-II;  [44]),

Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development 3rd edition (Bayley-3;
[45]),  and  Vineland  Adaptive  Behavior  Scales  [46].  See  S1  for  tools
identified in our first search (i.e., objective one) that did not meet the
inclusion criteria for objective two. See Table 1 for a summary of digital
child  developmental  tools  delivered  remotely,  a  description  of  their
digital  administration  (including  modifications  made  to  traditional

2.3. Digital ASQ findings

The  ASQ-2  was  the  focus  of  two  publications  examining  the  psy-
chometrics of remote digital delivery [37,39]. One paper permitted the
examination  of  equivalence  reliability  between  groups  [37]  and  be-
tween  group  equivalence  was  evaluated  in  the  other  [39].  Digital
methods  included  telephone  (i.e.,  interviewer  asked  the  parent  ques-
tions remotely over the phone) interviews [37] and remote computer-
based  administration,  where  parents  completed  an  electronic  version
of the ASQ-2 [39].

In one paper, authors  reported the concordance  (assessments con-
ducted an average of 11 days apart) between ASQ-2 12-month surveys
administered via telephone interview and parental self-administration
with  the  paper  version  [37].  Intraclass  correlation  coefficient  (ICC)
calculations  determined  that  the  greatest  agreement  between  survey
versions occurred with gross motor (ICC = 0.83), followed by commu-
nication (ICC = 0.76), personal-social (ICC = 0.50), fine motor (ICC =
0.43), and problem solving (ICC = 0.42) domains [37].

Between group equivalence was assessed with a quasi-experimental
evaluation comparing computer and paper ASQs [39]. The authors re-
ported no significant group differences (measured with independent t-
tests)  with  4-month  ASQs  but  found  that  children  whose  parents
completed the ASQs online had significantly lower 12 (e.g., communi-
cation, fine motor) and 24-month  (e.g., communication, gross motor)
scores  than  children  whose  parents  completed  the  paper  ASQs.  How-
ever, parent-child dyads were not randomized to group placement, and
demographic differences were observed between the web- and paper-
based groups. No differential functioning was found in overall ASQ-2
domains  (e.g.,  communication);  therefore,  the  authors  posited  that
group differences were due to differences in children’s development and
concluded  that  the  computer  and  paper  versions  were  generally

2.4. Revised Prescreening Denver Questionnaire (R-PDQ)

The  R-PDQ  is  a  parent  reported  tool  utilized  to  measure  motor,
language,  and  personal-social  scores  in  children  0–6  years  [43].  The
revised version was developed to expand screening ages, increase test
difficulty,  and  improve  comparisons  with  Denver  Developmental
Screening  Test  norms  [49].  The  R-PDQ  was  initially  evaluated  in  the
United States, and has been utilized in other countries, such as Canada
[37] and low- and middle-income countries [50].

2.5. Digital R-PDQ findings

Telephone administration of the 9–12 month R-PDQ, was compared
to in-person paper administration [37], which permitted the examina-
tion  of  equivalence  reliability.  An  ICC  was  not  reported  for  the  fine
motor scale as parents reported no fine motor delays with telephone or
paper administration. The highest ICC was reported for the gross motor
scale  (ICC = 0.90), followed  by language (ICC  = 0.57)  and personal-
social (ICC = 0.26) scales.

2.6. Bayley-3

The  Bayley-3  is  an  assessment  tool  that  measures  cognitive,  lan-
guage, motor, social-emotional, and adaptive behavior for children aged
1–42 months [45]. It requires 50–90 min for administration and a pro-
fessional (e.g., graduate training) to administer the exam [51]. During
COVID-19,  telehealth  practice  considerations  were  offered  [52].  The
Bayley-3 has been administered in varied cultures (e.g., [53,54]) and has
been cited as a gold standard assessment tool [55,56].

2.9. Digital NEPSY-II findings

Videoconferencing technology was utilized to administer NEPSY-II
[44]  in  one  study  [40]  which  permitted  the  examination  of  between
group equivalence. Due to COVID-19, the authors recruited families to
participate in a study with data collected online and compared the re-
sults  to  data  collected  in-person  the  year  prior.  In  the  online  study,
children born full-term and prematurely received the NESPY-II statue
(assesses  attention  and  executive  functioning)  and  NEPSY-II  affect
recognition (assesses social perception) subtests over videoconferencing
technology [40]. The NEPSY-II theory of mind and comprehension of
instructions subtests were not administered online due to internet con-
nectivity concerns as these subtests require that test instructions are not
repeated. No significant differences were observed between the online
and  in-person  group  (for  full-term  and  children  born  prematurely)  in
NEPSY-II statue or affect recognition scores.

2.10. Vineland adaptive behavior scales

The  Vineland  Adaptive  Behavior  Scales  measure  communication,
daily living, socialization, and motor skills [46]. The test, now available
in  its  third  edition,  is  utilized  for  individuals  from  birth  to  90  years
(Vineland-3; [60]). The tool is professionally administered (e.g., mas-
ter’s degree in psychology) and requires 10–40 min for completion [61].
While the Vineland-3 was not standardized with digital administration,
recommendations for telepractice are available [62]. The Vineland is the
oldest (and potentially best known) comprehensive, standardized mea-
sure of adaptive behavior [63].

2.7. Digital Bayley-3 findings

2.11. Digital vineland adaptive behavior scale findings

The Bayley-3 was evaluated digitally in one publication [41], with
findings contributing to knowledge on its between group equivalence
and inter-observer reliability. A subset of Bayley-3 motor, cognitive, and
language items were administered to infants through videoconferencing
technology after COVID-19 physical distancing restrictions commenced,
and these findings were compared to infants who previously received
the test in-person [41].

Although families were not randomized to the in-person or online
group, no significant differences were observed between groups based
on  demographic  measures  [41].  Videoconferencing  assessments  were
recorded  and  20  %  of  videos  were  assessed  with  a  second  observer;
concordance  (i.e.,  inter-observer  reliability)  ranged  from  0.82  to  1.0.
Infants  who  received  portions  of  the  Bayley-3  online  did  not  differ
significantly in language, cognitive, or motor scores compared to the in-
person group. The authors reported greater missing data (e.g., due to
audio, visual, or internet connectivity concerns) for the receptive lan-
guage and gross motor domains than the cognitive, expressive language,
and  fine  motor  domains  and  concluded  that  receptive  language  and
gross  motor  function  were  more  difficult  than  the  other  domains  to
administer digitally.

(NEPSY-II)

2.12. Digital administration considerations

We reviewed the included articles for contextual considerations on
remote digital tool administration. While none of these articles evalu-
ated the effectiveness of these themes, researchers, clinicians, and test
developers who plan to administer (and evaluate the psychometrics of)
multi-domain child development tools will likely benefit from the stra-
tegies  utilized  and  lessons  learned  in  these  studies.  We  identified  the
importance of examiner support in four articles [37,39–41]. Yovanoff
and colleagues [39] shared (anecdotally) that parents who completed
the  ASQ-2  on  their  computer  did  not  receive  professional  support  as
often  as  parents  who  completed  the  questionnaires  in  person,  which
could  affect  parental  understanding  of  items  and  item  responses.  Re-
searchers prepared families for videoconferencing sessions (e.g., camera
and child positioning, toys required, limiting distractions) and provided
feedback and guidance during meetings, such as how to administer tasks
to their child [40,41]. In one study, authors questioned whether social
desirability bias affected differences in parents’ direct report (i.e., tele-
phone  interview)  versus  independent  tool  completion  on  paper  [37].

Although strategies were not discussed to limit social desirability bias
[37], it is possible that this risk could be minimized through the provi-
sion of a nonjudgmental research environment.

Tool modification themes were identified in three studies. Given that
the Bayley-3 and NEPSY-II are typically administered in a standardized
laboratory setting with direct testing, it is not surprising that only spe-
cific  subtests  and  items  were  deemed  applicable  for  online  testing
[40,41]. Additionally, the receptive language and gross motor domains
were more challenging to administer remotely than the expressive lan-
guage, cognitive, and fine motor domains in one study [41]. In contrast,
Yovanoff  and  colleagues  [39]  questioned  whether  online  data  collec-
tions encouraged parents to complete the ASQ-2 while their child was
sleeping,  which  conflicts  with  normative  testing  procedures,  and
stressed  the  importance  of  providing  parents  with  instructions  to
observe their child while completing the questionnaire.

The theme of time was identified in three publications. When tran-
sitioning data collections online, findings from a focus group were uti-
lized to prevent screen fatigue by reducing videoconferencing meeting
times [40]. While families met with the research team for 3 h in person
(other tests were administered in addition to the NEPSY-II), four meet-
ings  were  scheduled  (30–45  min  each)  over  Zoom  [40].  In  another
study, online data collections provided flexibility, and researchers and
parents could postpone assessments for optimal times, such as when the
child was alert and quiet [41]. In contrast, in a web-based ASQ-2 eval-
uation, parents were limited to completing the ASQ-2 in 1 h [39]; while
ASQ testing is expected to require less than 1 h, time limits could affect
parents’  ability  to  observe  their  child’s  development  if  their  child  is
tired, hungry, or requiring a break.

Family resources and comfort promotion were identified in three and
two studies, respectively. Families utilized their own devices to partic-
ipate in videoconferencing meetings in one study [41] and were mailed
a  tablet,  if  needed,  in  another  [40].  In  two  studies,  resources  (e.g.,
internet  connectivity,  available  toys)  informed  decisions  about  which
items to administer online [40,41]. In another study, parents completed
ASQ-2 assessments on computers, yet the authors questioned whether
mobile devices (e.g., smartphones) would have promoted parental ca-
pacity to assess their child’s development while completing the surveys
[39].  Strategies  to  promote  comfort  included  encouraging  the  parent
and child to play for 5–10 min before beginning tasks [40] and attaining
parental consent before beginning video recordings [41]. Further, Gas-
parini  and  colleagues  questioned  whether  online  assessments  would
have been more difficult for parents with older, more mobile children, as
parents  administered  the  testing  items  while  ensuring  their  child
remained in the camera view [41].

3. Discussion

To  our  knowledge,  this  is  the  first  review  to  examine  the  psycho-
metric  properties  of  remote  digitally  delivered  existing  multi-domain
child  development  screening  and  assessment  tools  for  children  aged
0–5 years and to synthesize knowledge on contextual factors that can
affect their use. Of the 33 tools identified in our search (objective one),
we found five peer-reviewed publications comparing the digital delivery
of two screening (i.e., ASQ-2, R-PDQ) and three assessment (i.e., Bayley-
3, NEPSY-II, Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales) tools to traditional (e.
g.,  paper,  direct  in-person  testing)  administration  (objective  two).
Contextual considerations were reviewed (objective three); for example,
modifications were made to some tools and their delivery, such as only
administering subtests and/or items deemed appropriate for online de-
livery [40,41]. Other multi-domain child developmental tools have been
administered  digitally  due  to  the  COVID-19  pandemic,  such  as  the
DAYC-2 (e.g., [26,27]), and physical distancing restrictions encouraged
publishers  to  share  recommendations  for  administering  other  tools
digitally, such as the Speed Developmental Indicators for the Assessment
of Learning (DIAL)-4th edition [64,65]. COVID-19 motivated rapid in-
novations to minimize disruptions to health services and research, and

we expect that future research will broaden knowledge on the psycho-
metrics of other remote digitally delivered tools.

In this body of literature, single group study designs showed some
promise of equivalence between traditional and remote digitally deliv-
ered tools. Within-group equivalence reliability was evaluated with the
ASQ-2, R-PDQ [37], and Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales [38] with
telephone  and  in-person  administration.  The  Vineland  Adaptive
Behavior  Scales  demonstrated  high  equivalence  reliability  (ICC  =
0.96–0.99) across developmental domains [38], while the ASQ-2 (ICC =
0.42–0.83)  and  R-PDQ  (ICC  = 0.26–0.90)  [37]  showed  greater  vari-
ability. These results are similar to test-retest reliability findings of the
ASQ-3  in  another  study  (ICC  = 0.67–0.95),  where  parents  completed
questionnaires twice in a clinic waiting room [66]. Further, studies have
reported “difficult to interpret questions” on the R-PDQ [67], which may
explain  the  personal-social  R-PDQ  domain  ICC  of  0.26  in  Karam  and
colleagues’ [37] study. Although these studies show overall promise for
consistency  between  telephone  and  in-person  administration  of  child
development tools, additional research is needed to further these results.
Similarly, some evidence of equivalence was observed in studies that
evaluated child development tools administered digitally (in one sam-
ple)  and  compared  the  findings  to  another  sample  who  received  a
traditional format. Between group equivalence was evaluated in three
papers [39–41]. In one study, the authors concluded that the mode effect
(i.e., responses differ between assessment method) was not present with
web versus paper-based ASQ-2 administration [39]. No significant dif-
ferences were observed between groups who received NEPSY-II subtests
[40]  and  Bayley-3  items  [41]  with  videoconferencing  and  in-person
administration. While some differences were found, these findings are
similar to other emerging research showing equivalence between data
collected in-person and digitally with children, such as with the Still-
Face  Paradigm  [68],  and  with  language  [69]  and  cognitive  assess-
ments [70,71].

Overall, other reliability and validity measures were lacking in this
body  of  research.  Inter-observer  reliability  was  assessed  in  one  study
[41],  but  other  psychometric  analyses,  focusing  solely  on  digitally
delivered tools, were not found. Other authors have evaluated the val-
idity of remote digitally delivered tools but did not differentiate between
remote digital and other forms (e.g., paper and pencil) of administration
[72,73]; therefore, these studies were not included in our review. For
example,  the  ASQ-3  (assessed  online,  through  email,  and  in  person;
method not differentiated) was compared to in-person administration of
the Bayley-3 for infants who underwent cardiac surgery [72]; the ASQ-3
demonstrated  88  %  sensitivity  and  74  %  specificity  with  detecting
developmental  delays  at  12  months  and  74  %  sensitivity  and  88  %
specificity at 24 months when employing a (cid:0) 2 standard deviation below
average  cut-off  in  comparison  to  the  Bayley-3.  We  expect  that  other
psychometric publications (e.g., on predictive or structural validity) will
emerge with future utilization of digitally delivered child development
tools.  Further,  we  recommend  that  test  developers  share  norms  on
remote digital delivery as psychometric evaluations continue.

The third objective of this review was to explore contextual consid-
erations for the remote digital delivery of multi-domain child develop-
ment  tools.  We  identified  themes  related  to  examiner  support,  tool
modifications, time, family resources, and promotion of comfort. These
themes are also present in other research on remote digitally delivered
tools. For example, authors shared strategies utilized for administering
child  development  tools,  such  as  the  Mullen  Scales  of  Early  Learning
[74] online during COVID-19 [75].  The authors  described the impor-
tance of providing support to families (e.g., answering caregiver ques-
tions), necessity of adapting tools (e.g., eliminating items with complex
instructions  or  others  requiring  proprietary  materials),  time  (e.g.,
decreased  assessment  times,  removal  of  commuting  time),  family
resource constraints (e.g., internet speed), and promotion of comfort (e.
g.,  offering  breaks)  [75].  Similarly,  other  researchers  administered
spatial  and  visuo-motor  assessments  to  preschool  children  online  and
outlined the importance of communicating with parents to ensure they

were prepared, providing encouragement, promoting family comfort (e.
g.,  offering  instructions  without  overburdening  the  parent),  and  out-
lining  strategies  to  manage  technology  (e.g.,  utilizing  Wi-Fi  hotspot)
constraints [76].

3.1. Limitations

While  publications  are  emerging  on  digitally  delivered  tools,  this
area  of  research  is  nascent,  which  constrained  our  ability  to  deeply
investigate  contexts  and  mechanisms  affecting  the  psychometrics  and
delivery of these tools. We searched numerous databases and updated
our search, but it is possible that some studies (e.g., did not indicate that
tools were delivered digitally in title or abstract) were not found. As this
body of research is novel, we encourage future authors to explicate their
delivery method in the abstract and differentiate  between digital and
traditional delivery methods in their analyses. Further, unlike system-
atic reviews, realist reviews are not formulaic, which is beneficial for
investigating  social  interventions,  but  this  strategy  affects  reproduc-
ibility [30].

Additionally, these studies included families with relatively homo-
geneous demographics. For example, primarily White families with high
incomes and educational levels were included. Although research has
shown that the digital divide (i.e., inequitable access to the internet and
internet-enabled devices) is decreasing [77], not all families have access
to the internet and internet-enabled devices. For example, in an analysis
of 1,210,714 individuals in the United States, individuals in rural areas
were 2.07 times less likely to have access to a computer with high-speed
internet and 1.72 times less likely to have a smartphone with a data plan
than  individuals  in  metropolitan  areas  [78].  Thus,  device  size  (e.g.,
whether surveys are legible on a smartphone screen) and connectivity
(e.g.,  for  videoconferencing)  are  relevant  considerations;  researchers
and clinicians may consider offering additional resources (e.g., provi-
sion  of  tablet  with  data)  to  further  increase  child  developmental
screening  and  assessment  reach.  In  one  study,  child  prematurity  was
included  in  analyses  [40]  and  another  study  included  children  with
congenital  heart  defects  and  developmental  delays  [38].  Additional
research is needed to understand the psychometric properties of digi-
tally delivered tools in diverse (e.g., families with low SES, varied eth-
nicities, living in rural areas, children born preterm) populations.

Further, the study designs comprise a noteworthy limitation in this
body  of  research  as  none  of  the  between  group  analyses  employed
randomization.  Two  studies  did  not  observe  group  differences  in  de-
mographics and reported no significant differences in NEPSY-II [41] and
Bayley-3  [40] scores.  However,  significant  demographic  group differ-
ences were reported in one study where significant differences in ASQ-2
results  were  also  observed  [39].  Given  that  extensive  research  has
shown  associations  between  demographic  variables,  such  as  SES  and
ethnicity,  and  children’s  development  (e.g.,  [79,80]),  it  is  important
that future researchers employ randomized controlled trial designs to
eliminate  the  potential  for  group  differences  due  to  demographic
characteristics.

4. Conclusion

Overall, papers found within this review show promise for equiva-
lence between digital and traditional administration and highlight the
relevance of contextual factors to consider when delivering digital child
developmental tools remotely. Findings from within-group equivalence
reliability evaluations demonstrated high consistency for the Vineland
Adaptive  Behavior  Scales,  and  in  domains  (e.g.,  gross  motor)  for  the
ASQ-2 and R-PDQ. Between group equivalence was demonstrated be-
tween digital and in-person methods for subtests on the NEPSY-II and
items  on the Bayley-3, and a  between group evaluation  of the ASQ-2
determined  that  paper-pencil  and  web-based  questionnaires  were
approximately  equivalent.  Contextual  factors  relevant  to  digital
