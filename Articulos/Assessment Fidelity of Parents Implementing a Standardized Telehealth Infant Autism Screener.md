Telehealth Infant Autism Screener

Abstract
Background: Telehealth is effective for service delivery in pediatric occupational therapy across
ages and diagnoses. Remote parent coaching provides unique benefits for both parents and
infants. As a result of COVID-19, practitioners and researchers pivoted to remote assessment
and intervention without much preparation or training. It is critical that we evaluate the quality of
these telehealth services. One important component of remote evaluations is assessment fidelity.
Objectives: To examine assessment fidelity of a telehealth-delivered observational autism
screening tool for infants.
Methodology: An assessment fidelity checklist was applied as the primary outcome measure.
Results: Parents conducted assessments with 82% adherence to the fidelity checklist.
Implications: A parent coaching telehealth approach may be valid for assessment in pediatric
telehealth. Continually monitoring the assessment fidelity of a tool is critical for the valid
administration of remote services.

that its effectiveness may be comparable to in-person services (Önal et al., 2021; Camden
et al., 2020). Within pediatric settings, family-centered care is central to OT practice,
and parent coaching has been a successful method for maintaining family-centeredness
during remote OT service delivery (D’Arrigo et al., 2020; DeGrace, 2003; Camden et

convenient, collaborative, and empowering (Wallish et al., 2019). Although 84% of pediatric
OT practitioners expressed that telehealth services implemented during the pandemic were
effective, only 5% felt that comprehensive evaluations and assessments could be effectively

The COVID-19 pandemic re-defined healthcare service delivery, expanding the use of
telehealth services two-fold across healthcare professions (Koonin et al., 2020). During the
pandemic, OT practitioners played an essential role in telehealth service delivery, supporting
individuals across the lifespan to engage in meaningful occupations (Hoel et al., 2020;
American Occupational Therapy Association). OT telehealth services were implemented
among a variety of practice settings, including children & youth, developmental disabilities,
home & community health, rehabilitation & disability, and mental health (Abbott-Gaffney et
al., 2022). Due to the unexpected nature of COVID-19 and access to burgeoning telehealth
services, OT practitioners and researchers necessarily and rapidly pivoted to remote
administration of assessments and interventions, often without much training, direction,
or preparation (Dahl-popolizio et al., 2021). With OT practitioners continuing to deliver
telehealth services into the foreseeable future, it is imperative to evaluate the extent to which
a high standard of service delivery can be maintained in the telehealth context.
One component of high quality service delivery is valid administration of assessments.
Evaluation of assessment fidelity, or the extent to which the tool is administered
and scored the way that it was intended, is important to understand the degree to
which assessments are correctly and reliably administered (Grisham-Brown, Hallam, and
Pretti-Frontczak, 2008; Reed et. al, 2014). Assessment fidelity is critical for several
reasons. First, standardized assessment scores are valid only to the extent that the
assessment was administered in the same way as it was administered to normative
samples used to develop the scoring algorithms. Because OT practitioners and researchers
commonly use standardized assessment scores to make practice decisions and demonstrate
medical necessity for reimbursement, assessment fidelity can influence intervention
recommendations. Assessment fidelity can also increase accuracy and rigor of assessment
procedures through the improvement of training manuals and protocols (Richardson et al.,
2016; Reed & Sturges, 2012; Mazumdar & Donovan, 2020; Spell et al., 2020). Although
many studies have examined fidelity of intervention implementation (e.g., Hodges et al.,
2022; Gunderson, Symons, & Wolff, 2022), there are few that evaluate the fidelity of
assessment implementation either in person or via telehealth formats. Further, the bulk of the
existing literature is limited to assessments of aphasia and school-based performance (Reed
& Sturges, 2012; Reed et al., 2014; Spell et al., 2020; Richardson et al., 2016; Grisham-
Brown et al., 2008; Dekhtyar et al., 2020); only one study examined the assessment fidelity
within a pediatric telehealth context (Dekhtyar et al., 2020). This initial literature showed
that with clear administration guidelines and training procedures, telehealth assessment can
be reliable and valid (Dekhtyar et al., 2020).

Within pediatric telehealth practice, parent coaching is an effective and high-quality
approach to remote service delivery (Yllades et al., 2022). Parent mediated approaches
allow caregivers to learn and implement evidenced-based strategies in the context of
their own homes and routines, while also improving independence and carryover of
strategies long term (Vismara et al., 2012). Moreover, effective parent coaching leads
to improved caregiver engagement, responsiveness, and reflection and reduced parental
stress, in addition to improving children’s communication skills and participation in daily
routines (Rush et al., 2003; Siller et al., 2018; Estes et al., 2014; Moore et al, 2014).
Parent coaching is most effective when it includes the following components: real-time
learning experiences, collaborative decision making, modeling, immediate feedback, and
opportunities for reflection (Pellecchia et al., 2022). When comparing in-person to telehealth
pediatric parent coaching interventions, telehealth intervention fidelity is maintained and
even improves when delivered alongside in-depth training and routine fidelity checks
(Carson et al., 2022; Roben et al, 2022). Although parent coaching has been shown to
be effective, it is rarely used among early intervention (EI) providers (Campbell & Coletti,
2013; Douglas et al., 2020). It is essential that practitioners, especially those in EI, are
trained in parent coaching and educated on the unique benefits of this model in remote
service delivery.
While most of the literature on remote parent coaching has focused on implementation
of early intervention, there is some emerging work regarding parent coaching assessment
procedures. These include translation and adaption of existing diagnostic tools for autism,
such as the Brief Observation of Symptoms in Autism (BOSA; Lord et al., 2020) and the
Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2; Lord et al., 2012) as
well as the development of new tools for diagnosis and developmental assessment, such
as the Tele-ASD-PEDS (TAP; Corona et al, 2020). See Berger et al. 2022 for a review
of these tools. The Telehealth Evaluation of Development for Infants (TEDI; Talbott et al.
2020, 2021) is a parent coaching protocol for conducting a social communication-focused
developmental assessment for infants. In the full protocol, parents are coached through a
series of semi-structured play activities designed to allow for the scoring of standardized
tools, including the Autism Observation Scale for Infants (AOSI; Bryson et al., 2008) and
the P-ESDM Infant-Toddler Curriculum Checklist (ITCC; Rogers et al., 2021). See Talbott
et. al., 2021 for a more detailed description of the play activities and protocol. Initial pilot
studies of the TEDI reported high inter-rater agreement (ICC’s of .94 and .89) and low to
moderate test-retest reliability (r = .46 and .25) for the AOSI and the ITCC in a sample of
6-12-month-olds with early social communication concerns (Talbott et al., 2021).
The primary purpose of this study was to validate telehealth administration of a standardized
pediatric assessment using a parent coaching model. More specifically, we aim to examine
the assessment fidelity of the AOSI for infants within a remote context. Here, we extend
previous work on telehealth administration of the AOSI in an independent community
sample of infants recruited without specific concern for autism or other developmental
disorders. We hypothesized that the assessment fidelity will be acceptable.

Methods
Participants
Data for this study were drawn from an ongoing study that recruits families nationwide for
telehealth participation from their homes. The broader aim of the study was to examine
individual differences in social communication and sensory regulatory behaviors in infants
between 6 and 16 months of age from a community sample in the United States. The
study used the First Years Inventory, version 3.1 (FYI; Baranek et al., 2013; Baranek et
al., 2022) as a parent-report screener to recruit and stratify children according to various
risk levels of social communication and sensory-regulatory functions. Parent-infant dyads
were then further assessed with a larger clinical battery of assessments using the telehealth
format. Available data from 28 families were analyzed for this paper. Data were drawn
from one observational assessment, the Autism Observation Scale for Infants (AOSI; Bryson
& Zwaigenbaum, 2014). Participants were recruited through California vital records with
informational materials sent to families through the mail, as well as through word of mouth
and social media. Eligibility criteria for families were: (a) having an infant ages 6-16
months with no known genetic conditions, significant medical problems (e.g. seizures),
or significant uncorrected vision/hearing/physical impairments; (b) speaking primarily (i.e.
≥50% of the time) English or Spanish in the home; and (c) having sufficient fluency in
English to participate in study procedures and coaching and complete questionnaires. All
28 participants reported being native or very fluent English speakers. See Table 1 for
characteristics of the sample.
Procedures
All study procedures were approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB),
the Ethics Committee was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and participants
completed informed consent procedures prior to starting study activities. Sessions were
recorded via Zoom, and participants were compensated $50 for their time via an electronic
transfer or gift card. Families were mailed kits with assessment materials and provided with
a return label pre-paid by the study to send the kits back to the lab following the visit.
Measures
The primary measure used was the Autism Observation Scale for Infants (AOSI; Bryson
& Zwaigenbaum, 2014). The AOSI is a 19-item direct observational measure that includes
a set of standardized activities intended to distinguish early autism behaviors in 6-18-month-
olds. It has been used in both research and clinical contexts (Zwaigenbaum et al., 2021;
Brian et al., 2008). Visual tracking, attention disengagement, orientation to name, social
babbling, coordination of eye gaze and action, sensory reactivity, and motor control and
behavior, among other target behaviors, are assessed through play-based structured and
unstructured activities. Nine items are scored based on specific, structured activities and/or
presses administered by the assessor, with the remainder scored through observations
made throughout the assessment, which includes two free play periods. Item scores range
from 0 to 3, with higher scores representing more atypical behavior (0=typical behavior;
1=inconsistent, questionable, subtle behavior; 2=atypical behavior; 3=extremely atypical
behavior) (Bryson et al., 2008). Both a total score and a marker count are computed.

Higher total scores (sum of all item scores; maximum=44) indicate an increased presence
of atypical behaviors that may be associated with a later autism diagnosis. Scores ≥7 at 6
months of age, ≥6 at 9 months of age, ≥7 at 12 months of age, and ≥10 at 15 months of
age are indicative of elevated likelihood for autism (Zwaigenbaum et al., 2021). A “marker”
is assigned to all non-zero item scores (1, 2, 3; maximum=16). Previous studies have
demonstrated inter-rater reliability of .90 and test-retest reliability of .63 in a 12-month-old
sample (Bryson et al., 2008). Inter-rater reliability for the AOSI total scores and total
marker count was 0.94 (95% CI 0.78, 0.98) and 0.95 (95% CI 0.82, 0.99), respectively. For
remote administration of the AOSI, we used a detailed parent coaching protocol adapted
from Talbott and colleagues (2020). In this protocol, one item was excluded: differential
response to facial emotion, which codes the infant’s response to the assessor’s deliberate
change from a positive to neutral facial emotion. This item was excluded during initial
development due to limitations of the telehealth context, such as prioritizing a camera set-up
that allows for a full view of the infant’s face, rather than the parent, resulting in the assessor
having difficulty identifying when the parent was pressing the item, subsequently affecting
their ability to score the emotion change. Of the items administered via telehealth, nine
are directly elicited using specific administration protocols, while the others are observed
throughout the assessment and not based on specified administration protocols. Assessment
fidelity was only examined for the nine items that are directly elicited. Additionally,
explicitly coaching parents to gain the infant’s attention often negatively impacted the
spontaneity of the interaction and naturalistic assessment environment.
In the current study, parents played an active role in the session, directly administering the
activities through detailed coaching by a trained assessor. Assessors were female OTs with
research training for administration of the AOSI, experience observing early signs of autism,
and involvement in the coaching and scoring of the assessment. The primary purpose of
the protocol was for parents to initiate structured and unstructured activities that informed
the assessor’s scoring of the infant’s skills and early signs of autism. A general script was
provided for activities alongside suggestions for session set-up, opening, parent positioning,
closing, and troubleshooting. All assessors received training on the implementation of the
protocol.
The assessment fidelity checklist was developed for the purposes of this study to measure
the assessment fidelity of the remote administration of the AOSI. Assessment fidelity was
the primary outcome for this study. This checklist outlines several critical administration
fidelity indicators for each of the nine structured AOSI items. Each fidelity indicator was
assigned a score of 0 (no) or 1 (yes), with a total fidelity score calculated as the number
of indicators met, divided by the total number of indicators (e.g., 14 indicators met/31
indicators total=45% fidelity). Two trained raters independently applied the fidelity checklist
to Zoom recordings of parent-administered AOSI assessments. Agreement (intraclass
correlation coefficient) between the two raters on the assessment fidelity checklist was 0.84.
Demographic questionnaire.—Participants completed an online demographic survey
developed for this study to gather information regarding the parent’s race/ethnicity and
education level.

Data Analyses
Electronic data on all parent-report surveys were collected in Qualtrics XM (Qualtrics,
Provo, UT). Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 (IBM Corp, 2020)
was used for all data analyses. Descriptive analyses of demographic variables included
mean and standard deviation calculations for continuous variables, as well as percentage
calculations for categorical variables to characterize the sample (see Table 1).
Results
Parents, on average, conducted assessments with 82% fidelity (SD = 6%), with a minimum
of 71% and maximum of 93% across fidelity indicators. The following indicators had the
lowest fidelity: presenting the soft book last in free play 1 (29%), administering the second
name call 5 seconds after the first if the infant did not respond on the first trial (39%),
using the child’s shoulders as outer limits during visual tracking (46%), presenting the ball
last in free play 2 (46%), and discontinuing an imitation of an action after child passes an
attempt (50%). Fidelity percentages ranged from 29 to 100%, with 23 out of 31 indicators
at or above 75%. See Table 2 for the complete list of fidelity percentages by indicator and
associated AOSI item.
The AOSI total score range in our sample was 1-15, with a mean of 6.3 and standard
deviation of 3.6, and the total marker count range was 1-9 with a median of 4. We
categorized this sample into age bands of 6-8 months, 9-11 months, 12-14 months, and
>15 months to apply clinical cutoffs for elevated likelihood of autism. Nine children scored
above the cutoff in their age band.
Discussion
This study examined the assessment fidelity of an autism screening tool for infants ages
6-16 months conducted via telehealth parent coaching. Parent assessment fidelity was found
to be quite strong (82%), supporting validity of administration of the AOSI via parent
coaching. Compared to the literature on fidelity of direct in-person assessment by trained
examiners (90%; Wanzek & Roberts, 2012), the indirect telehealth method of coaching
parents as assessors in our study fared slightly lower. This was expected, given that
clinicians and researchers typically undergo intensive training for in-person assessment and
scoring. Parents in this telehealth context only received brief coaching immediately prior
to administering assessment items, and their fidelity scores may also be affected to some
degree by the varied coaching style and performance across assessors. While assessment
fidelity is important for trustworthy administration of standardized assessments and use of
assessment results (Richardson et al., 2016; Reed & Sturges, 2012; Mazumdar & Donovan,
2020; Spell et al., 2020), maintaining rapport with children and parents during telehealth
administration of any assessment is key to promote optimal engagement in a therapeutic
clinical or research context (Cason et al., 2018). Such rapport may sometimes come at the
cost of assessment fidelity, given that it is not always favorable to press for re-administration
of items that were not administered to fidelity, due to child and parent frustration and time
constraints. Additionally, standard assessment procedures only allow for a limited number
of re-administrations. Nonetheless, the level of assessment fidelity obtained in this study for

Telehealth Assessment Troubleshooting
It may be important for clinicians to consider how videoconferencing features, such
as automatically muting loud sounds, can affect ability to gain an accurate picture of
a child’s skills in a telehealth format. For example, some items are difficult to score
from a remote perspective due to subtlety of the behavior or difficulties maintaining
consistent audio quality via Zoom. Such items are already complex in nature, and the added
limitations of the telehealth format may exacerbate scoring challenges. Various supportive
strategies were used to minimize potential telehealth barriers with regard to technology,
assessment standardization, distractions, and variability of resources. In efforts to combat
these challenges, it is important to consider the positioning of the parent and child during
the session. Having a clear view of the child’s face and hands as well as the parent’s face
helped to ensure an adequate view of the child’s behavior and to reduce the likelihood of the
child becoming distracted by the screen on the device used to connect to telehealth services.
To minimize technological glitches, parents received a detailed troubleshooting guide one
week prior to the session that outlined practical solutions to common audio, visual, and
network issues; audio and visual input was tested prior to starting the session; and a protocol
was established for use during disruptions to the teleconferencing audio. To increase the
standardization of the AOSI protocol via telehealth, we created a standardized coaching
script, implemented standardized training materials and reliability checks to increase fidelity
of parent coaching, and noted all protocol deviations. To minimize distractions during the
session, clear expectations for session space and device set-up were communicated to the
parent, such as removing all extra toys from the designated assessment area, dimming the
screen brightness on the device, and placing the device up high or out of sight. To account
for variability of resources among families, assessment supplies and compatible devices
were shipped to participants’ homes and returned upon session completion.
Through the administration of this parent coaching pediatric assessment via telehealth,
we gained valuable insight into the capabilities of parents as assessors, with parents
achieving high overall levels of fidelity. Even for specific indicators with lower fidelity,
examiners were still able to observe behaviors, while also maintaining rapport with
families. Surprisingly, two of the indicators with the lowest fidelity on average consisted
of administration procedures pressed during the unstructured free play periods, which
potentially speaks to the parents’ strong ability to implement more structured, concrete
activities during parent coaching, rather than those that are more vague, such as the order
of toy presentation during a free play period. This is encouraging and speaks to the strong
potential of telehealth administration using a uniform parent coaching script to implement
standardized, structured pediatric OT assessments.

the assessor was looking for. For example, during the imitation of actions item, parents
tended to provide their children with more opportunities to imitate than was specified by
the coach. To address this, the assessors provided parents with additional instruction and
feedback following each trial, often orienting caregivers to the number of trials remaining
or the timing thereof. Additionally, examiners discovered that parents were more likely to
administer items correctly when coaching was individually tailored and adjusted to best
support their level of understanding. Examiners used a variety of communication modes,
including verbal description and instruction as well as live visual demonstration and video
demonstration for one particularly complex item. The study team displayed a video on
screen to provide parents with a demonstration of correct administration prior to coaching
by the examiner, a strategy which we recommend for complex, highly structured assessment
items. A concise coaching script using lay language and assessment items clearly organized
and labeled were important strategies for improving assessment fidelity and overall caregiver
experience. In addition to learning effective coaching strategies, procedures for this study
(e.g., calculating the assessment fidelity) helped the study team to elucidate consistently
problematic items, allowing for appropriate alterations to coaching and training procedures
to promote increased assessment fidelity and scoring reliability in the ongoing larger study.
Based on lessons learned in this study, we recommend that OT practitioners and
researchers regularly examine fidelity of their telehealth assessment procedures. High
quality administration of standardized assessment tools is key to support accurate results
and interpretations that inform clinical practice and research findings. Moreover, the
implementation of regular trainings on fidelity and scoring reliability is an important
component to building consensus among several assessors, promoting increased confidence
in assessment results.
Strengths & Limitations
This study included a diverse sample of families with 6-16 month old infants recruited from
southern California who had access to devices supporting internet and Zoom conferencing.
Implementation of remote administration procedures allowed for inclusion of diverse
families across a wide geographic catchment area and with less burden than required
for parents who must drive for their clinical and/or research appointments. The study’s
innovative methods and novel findings fill a gap in the current literature regarding the
validity of telehealth assessment in OT practice and research, and further validate parent
coaching approaches to telehealth assessment with the pediatric population. Although
COVID-19 necessitated telehealth procedures, the pandemic experience uncovered the
potential of telehealth assessments to yield valid interpretations. In addition to valid results,
these procedures could provide access to high-quality services for individuals living in rural
or underserved communities.
Results should also be considered in light of the following limitations. The study lacked
a direct comparison group of infants assessed via in-person administration of the AOSI,
but rather used existing literature to compare fidelity scores across these two contexts.

Conclusions
This study sought to validate the remote administration of a pediatric autism screening
tool utilizing a parent coaching model. Although parent assessment fidelity was slightly
lower than the in-person examiner training standard, it was within the acceptable range.
Continually monitoring the assessment fidelity of a tool is critical, as it helps to
improve assessment training and promote increased agreement among assessors. Given
the importance of assessment fidelity, clinicians may want to consider building additional
assessment administration training into in-service and informal mentoring contexts to
promote delivery of assessments as intended, especially when preparing to coach parents
through telehealth. This study is the first to examine this aspects of assessment in
telehealth pediatric occupational therapy with an autism-specific observational assessment.
Future research directions include 1) examination of the relationship between assessment
fidelity and scoring reliability, 2) comparison of in-person vs. telehealth administration
of assessments with the same sample, and 3) development of telehealth adaptations of
assessment tools that typically require larger sets of standardized materials, which may
be difficult to distribute to families if they are costly or not widely available and easily
replaceable if lost. The COVID-19 pandemic forced a sudden transition to telehealth for
OT practitioners across contexts, and this study provides valuable insight into the potential
utility of a parent coaching model of remote assessment administration, which shows
promise for continued use of telehealth beyond the pandemic context.
