Caregiver Perspectives on the Use of Tele-Assessment for
Toddlers with Developmental Concerns

Abstract
Purpose: Most telehealth studies have focused on caregiver satisfaction with autism spectrum
disorder (ASD) assessments. Few have examined caregiver perceptions of direct-to-home
telehealth evaluations for children with developmental concerns broader than ASD.
Methods: The current study explored caregiver perspectives (N=439) of the telehealth process,
including technology barriers and satisfaction with remote delivery of diagnostic feedback, before
and after a 24-month tele-assessment, in children from four outcome groups (ASD, Speech-
Language Concerns, Other Developmental Concerns, and No Concerns).
Results: The results indicated overall high levels of caregiver satisfaction and acceptability
across all outcome groups, although the ASD group had a lower rate (89%) than the other
groups (99–100%) of rating “very true” to feeling like a valued partner in the tele-assessment, the
evaluation meeting their needs (89% v. 94–99%), and understanding the results (86% v. 96–98%).
Families of children with Speech-Language Concerns, the largest diagnostic outcome group, were
highly satisfied and found tele-assessment acceptable. There were no differences in caregiver
perceptions of the tele-assessment process based on whether they had prior concerns about
their child or not. Few technology barriers were reported by any groups and socio-demographic
variables (race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, travel distance from medical center) were not
associated with differences in technology access or satisfaction with the tele-evaluations.
Conclusion: Overall, these findings support the use of tele-assessment for a wide range of
children whose families may not have access to in-person assessment services, as well as suggest
ways to further improve remote models of care.

There are a variety of neurodevelopmental conditions evident in the first years of life,
including delays in the acquisition of developmental milestones in speech, social, motor,
and cognitive domains. Approximately 20% of parents with children under the age of 5
express concerns about their child’s development (Marshall et al., 2016), but they often face
delays in accessing diagnostic and intervention services (Marshall et al., 2020). Much of the
research in this area has been focused on children for whom concerns have been raised about
autism spectrum disorder (ASD), a condition with reported prevalence of 1 in 31 children
(3.2%) in the United States (Shaw et al., 2025). Studies have documented significant
difficulties in accessing initial diagnostic assessments, including long waiting lists, shortage
of trained professionals, over-burdened systems, cultural and linguistic barriers, and lengthy
travel to service sectors (Alfuraydan et al., 2020; Bishop-Fitzpatrick & Kind, 2017; Gordon-
Lipkin et al., 2016; Kanne & Bishop, 2021). There is an average delay of 2–4 years from
first concerns to the age of ASD diagnosis (Crane et al., 2015; Shaw et al., 2025; Zuckerman
et al., 2015). With greater awareness of ASD symptoms within the first few years of life
(Ozonoff et al., 2010; Salgado-Cacho et al., 2021; Tanner & Dounavi, 2021), coupled with
the benefits of earlier intervention (Guthrie et al., 2023), it is critical to find ways to reduce
this diagnostic odyssey.
Another group facing service access barriers in the first few years are children demonstrating
delays in their speech and language development. Within the United States, it is estimated
that one in eight children between the ages of 2 and 5 have a speech or language delay (Siu,
2015), with parents often becoming concerned when speech fails to emerge in the second or
third year of life (Paul & Roth, 2011). The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends
developmental screening at all well-child visits and surveillance for developmental concerns
including speech and language skills (Lipkin et al., 2020). Several tools are available
for assessing language development in toddlers and it is possible to identify delays by
18–24 months of age through screening (Dale et al., 2003; Jackson et al., 2023). Early
identification is critical because children with untreated speech or language delays are at
increased risk for later adverse school-age outcomes such as social isolation, academic
problems, and mental health issues (Dale et al., 2003; Jackson et al., 2023; Leslie et al.,
2007; Lipkin et al., 2020).
Telehealth assessments (i.e., approaches that enable families to receive professional services
and support at a distance) were utilized before the COVID-19 pandemic (Ellison et al.,
2021; Juárez et al., 2018; Soares & Langkamp, 2012) but came into much wider use
with pandemic-related restrictions on travel and in-person activities (Alfuraydan et al.,
2020; Dahiya et al., 2020; Wallis et al., 2021). Studies have shown that telehealth is an
effective alternative to traveling long distances, reduces access disparities in underserved
areas, improves identification-to-intervention pathways, and is generally acceptable to both
families and providers (Alfuraydan et al., 2020; Crane et al., 2015; Juárez et al., 2018;
Kryszak et al., 2022; Stavropoulos et al., 2022; Talbott et al., 2022a; Wagner et al.,
2023). Telehealth assessments can include synchronous (real-time video conferencing)
or asynchronous (store-and-forward) approaches that vary by setting (Nazneen et al.,
2015; Riva et al., 2023). Synchronous “clinic-based” tele-assessments occur outside of the
family home in local schools or regional clinics, with experts observing remotely, whereas
synchronous “direct-to-home” tele-assessments use the family home with caregivers and

Currently, the majority of telehealth studies focus on perceptions of and satisfaction with
ASD assessments in children for whom ASD concerns have been raised (Alfuraydan et al.,
2020; Jones et al., 2022; Stavropoulos et al., 2022; Wagner, Weitlauf, et al., 2021). Only
a few have examined the feasibility of telehealth for children with broader developmental
concerns beyond ASD (Keehn et al., 2022; Talbott et al., 2022b; Valentine et al., 2021).
In a recent systematic review, families and providers reported high levels of feasibility,
acceptability, and accuracy of “clinic-based” telehealth methods in facilitating a diagnosis
for children with non-ASD developmental concerns (e.g., fetal alcohol syndrome, speech
and language delays, genetic syndromes, etc.; La Valle et al., 2022). Of note, however, none
of the studies in this systematic review explored telehealth use within the home setting.
Despite improved early identification practices, there continue to be disparities between
expression of parental first concerns and receipt of diagnostic assessments (Marshall et
al., 2020; Schmidt et al., 2024). Telehealth evaluations may reduce this lag by offering
additional avenues for services, particularly in rural or underserved communities. It is
critical to investigate caregiver acceptance of telehealth assessments for a wide range of
developmental concerns (Juárez et al., 2018; Kellom et al., 2023; Schmidt et al., 2024),
which may in the future accelerate the pathway to diagnosis and intervention for many
families. In evaluating tele-assessment services, it is also important to examine whether
families residing in areas far from a major medical center, from culturally or linguistically
diverse communities, or of lower socio-economic status may have difficulty accessing
technology and telehealth. It has been hypothesized that families with limited broadband
access or technological literacy may be doubly disadvantaged by a “digital divide” when
trying to access services offered via telehealth (Chang et al., 2021; Gallegos-Rejas et al.,
2023).
The current investigation examined caregiver perceptions of direct-to-home tele-assessments
for children with and without ASD and broader developmental concerns (e.g., speech-
language delays, global developmental delays, behavioral problems) in a community-based
sample that was recruited early in life, prior to the mean age of most parental concerns
(Lipkin et al., 2020). The sample is unique in not being recruited for developmental
concerns or elevated likelihood of a neurodevelopmental condition, providing insights into
general caregiver experiences of tele-assessments. The primary research aim was to examine
potential differences in caregiver satisfaction, perceptions, and evaluation experiences across
a range of potential developmental delays.

participate in a longitudinal study from 6 to 36 months of age. Caregivers completed
online screening questionnaires every six months. If a participant screened positive on any
instrument at any age, families were offered a diagnostic tele-assessment appointment when

autism, speech, behavior challenges, etc.). Since developmental screeners often produce

actually have any concerns. Either way, you will be given some developmental feedback
and, if applicable, recommendations to help your child.” Immediately prior to and after the
tele-visit, caregivers completed questionnaires related to satisfaction with and acceptability
of the tele-assessment procedures.
Families were provided in advance of the visit with a one-page document describing the
procedures and suggested materials (e.g., books, shape sorters, musical toys, bubbles or
balls, cars, etc.) to have on hand for the tele-assessment. To reduce the burden on families,
they were encouraged to use items found within their homes and not purchase anything
specifically for the evaluation. Tele-assessment was completed via Zoom and generally
lasted between 1.5–2.5 hours. After the telehealth instruments were administered, the
virtual appointment was paused while the examiner scored assessments and formulated
their diagnostic impression. Examiners then rejoined the virtual visit, delivered feedback
to families, answered questions, and provided recommendations and resources based on
the child’s diagnosis/outcome. Following the tele-assessment, examiners provided reports to
families documenting clinical outcomes and recommendations.
Participants
Participants were recruited via hospital records, state birth records, and media
advertisements (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, and Google). Families were eligible to
participate in the study if they had: a) a child under the age of 10 months, b) a valid
email address, c) access to a device with internet capacity, and d) the ability to complete
questionnaires at a 5th grade English reading level. Participants were not excluded based on
any prior genetic or medical conditions, family history (e.g., elevated likelihood of ASD,
ADHD, multiple enrolled siblings, etc.), or prematurity. This recruitment procedure resulted
in an initial sample of 2,005 eligible participants.
If a participant screened positive on any instrument at any age (n=955; 47.6%), families
were offered a diagnostic tele-assessment appointment when their child was 24 months of
age. An additional 5.1% (n=102) of the participants who did not screen positive on any
instrument at any age were also invited for a tele-assessment to keep examiners naïve to
screening status. The remaining 47.3% of the sample (n=948) did not screen positive, was
not invited for a diagnostic tele-assessment, and only participated in the study by completing
online screeners. The current analyses focus on tele-assessment at 24 months of age. Of the
955 screen-positive participants offered tele-assessment, 697 (73.0%) completed a 24-month
tele-visit, 42 (4.4%) declined the invitation, and 216 (22.6%) were unreachable or unable to

Measures
Online Screeners (6–24 months)
Modified-Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised (M-CHAT-R). The M-CHAT-R
(Robins et al., 2014) is a parent-report checklist with 20 yes/no items normed for use
with toddlers 16 to 30 months of age. A positive screen is defined as three or more failed
items. A meta-analysis reported a pooled sensitivity of 83% (95% CI: 77–88%) and a
pooled specificity of 94% (95% CI: 89–97%) (Wieckowski et al., 2023). The M-CHAT-R
questionnaire was completed by families when their child was 18 and 24 months of age.
Infant-Toddler Checklist (ITC). The ITC (Wetherby et al., 2008) is a parent report
questionnaire about social-communication, language, and symbolic development. It yields
three composite scores (Social, Speech, and Symbolic) and a total score. It is normed for
use with children 6 to 24 months of age. A positive screen for communication and/or social
delays is defined as a score in the 10th percentile or lower on a composite or the Total score.
Caregivers completed the ITC at 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months.
Video-Referenced Infant Rating System for Autism (VIRSA). The VIRSA (Young et al.,
2020) is a video-based screening tool that presents pairs of videos of parents and infants
playing together. Videos depict a range of social-communication ability, and scores range
from one (least social) to 10 (most social). The tool requires forced-choice judgments
in which parents select the video from the pair that is most similar to how their child
behaves. The VIRSA has been shown to have satisfactory psychometric properties (test-
retest reliability, convergent validity) and the ability to detect ASD in a sample of elevated
familial likelihood of ASD (Young et al., 2020). Caregivers completed the VIRSA when
their child was 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months and a positive screen on the VIRSA is defined as
a score of three or below.

Parent Concerns Questionnaire (Ozonoff et al., 2009). Prior to the 24-month tele-
assessment visit, caregivers were asked if they had concerns about their child’s behavior
or development. Caregivers provided yes/no responses to six categories of concern

TELE-ASD-PEDS (TAP; Corona et al., 2021). The TAP is an interactive tool used to
assess behaviors associated with ASD in toddlers via two-way video conferencing, with
the child and caregiver(s) in their home while the examiner observes and gives directions
remotely. It consists of eight activities: free play and joint play, physical play routines (e.g.,
peekaboo, tickling), social responsivity (i.e., both name calling and directing attention),
activities that may prompt a child to request (e.g., snack), an anticipation-building activity
(e.g., blowing bubbles or rolling a ball back and forth), and planned ignoring. The TAP
capitalizes on materials typically found within a family home and if a particular item
is not available (e.g., bubbles), others are easily substituted (e.g., a ball). Immediately
following administration, examiners rate the child’s behavior on seven distinct behavioral
anchors (i.e., socially directed speech and sounds; frequent and flexible eye contact;
unusual vocalizations; unusual or repetitive play; unusual or repetitive body movements;
combines gestures, eye contact, and speech/vocalization; unusual sensory exploration or
reaction). Scoring utilizes a Likert scale (3=behaviors characteristic of ASD clearly present;
2=possible atypical behavior; 1=behaviors characteristic of ASD not present). Total scores
range from seven to 21, with cutoff scores of 12 or higher indicative of ASD. This diagnostic
tool has been validated for tele-assessment purposes and has high correspondence with
in-person evaluation results (Gangi et al., 2025).

Clinical interview. Examiners completed an interview with parents/caregivers that

Diagnostic Classification and Feedback. The mean age of the sample at the time of
the diagnostic tele-assessment was 23.9 months (SD=0.5 months; range 22.1 to 27.0
months). At the end of the 24-month tele-assessment, examiners who were kept unaware
of the participant’s screening status classified participants into one of four mutually
exclusive outcome groups: autism spectrum disorder (n=28), Speech-Language Concerns
(n=85), Other Developmental Concerns (n=39), or No Concerns (n=287). All examiners
had master’s or doctoral degrees and training and extensive experience in developmental
assessment and ASD evaluation in young children. ASD diagnoses were confirmed by
a licensed clinical psychologist via direct observation at the time of the visit. The ASD
classification was defined as a TAP score of ≥ 12 (Wagner et al., 2021) and meeting
DSM-5 ASD criteria based on all information gathered, including a clinical interview
with caregivers. Children were classified in the Speech-Language Concerns (SLC) group
if parents reported them to have fewer than 20 spoken words, if they received a below
average score on the DP-4 Communication scale, and/or were observed using few spoken
words during the 24-month tele-assessment. Participants were classified into the Other
Developmental Concerns (ODC) group based on behaviors observed during the 24-month
tele-assessment (e.g., high activity level, behavioral outbursts, defiance, age-inappropriate
shyness or social hesitance, etc.), clinical interviewing with families, and/or below average
scores on multiple DP-4 scales (e.g., global developmental delay). All other participants
were classified as No Concerns (NC). To be given an outcome of ASD, children were
required to meet DSM-5 criteria. All other clinical outcomes (e.g., within the ODC
and SLC groups) were not intended to meet specific DSM-5 criteria, but rather reflect
developmental concerns based on expert clinical judgment. Regardless of outcome, all
families were provided with feedback based on the evaluation results as well as a written
report documenting specific recommendations, if applicable. If challenges beyond ASD,
such as speech or global delays, were evident during the telehealth assessment (e.g., through
clinical observation, parent report, and/or DP-4 scores), parents were given verbal and
written feedback and provided with resources and ways to access support (e.g., state early
intervention system, public or private insurance). If a diagnosis was suspected but could not
be confirmed via telehealth, referral for further evaluation might also be made, although
this was infrequent, in part due to long waitlists for in-person assessments during and after
the COVID-19 pandemic. In rare instances, such as when families questioned evaluation
outcomes, a second feedback session with the additional participation of the supervising
licensed psychologist was offered to the family.

Results
Caregiver Perspectives before the 24-month Tele-Assessment
Children were, on average, 24.5 months (SD = 1.2) when their caregivers completed the
pre-visit questionnaire.
Familiarity Prior to Tele-Assessment Evaluation: On the three questions about
familiarity with typical child development, developmental milestones, and ASD, most
caregivers rated themselves as being at least a little to moderately familiar with these
concepts. There were no significant differences among the four outcome groups in
familiarity ratings (see Table 2).
Concerns Prior to Tele-Assessment Evaluation: The questionnaire then had
caregivers endorse whether, before the visit, they experienced any of six specific concerns
(e.g., about technology, procedures, receiving a diagnosis, etc.; see Table 2). Results
indicated significant differences among the four outcome groups on two of the six items: “I
am concerned my child will get a diagnosis of ASD” (χ2(3) = 9.79, p = .02), and “I am
concerned my child will not get a diagnosis of ASD” (χ2(3) = 11.15, p = .01), with the ASD
group being the most concerned relative to the other three groups (see Table 2).
The pre-visit questionnaire ended with two general questions about concerns. There were
significant differences found among the outcome groups (χ2(3) = 30, p < .001) in how
worried caregivers were about the impending evaluation. Post-hoc comparisons using
Dunn’s method with a Bonferroni correction for multiple tests indicated that the caregivers
in the ASD outcome group were significantly more worried about the evaluation than
the caregivers in the NC group (p < .01) and the SLC group (p < .01). There were no
differences found among the SLC, ODC, and NC groups (all ps > .05). There were also
significant differences among the outcome groups in whether the caregivers believed that
their child had ASD (χ2(3) = 107.6, p < .001). Post-hoc comparisons corrected for multiple

Caregiver Perspectives after the 24-month Tele-Assessment
The average age of the child when the post-visit questionnaire was completed was 24.6
months (SD = 1.3).
Examiner-Caregiver Partnership: Table 3 summarizes the questionnaire results in
which caregivers rated (from a scale of “very true” to “not true”) three statements regarding
their partnership with the examiner during the tele-assessment and six statements regarding
satisfaction with the procedures and feedback from the visit. The vast majority (over 89%)
of caregivers in all four outcome groups gave ratings of “very true” to all three statements
about the examiner-caregiver partnership. There were, however, statistically significant
differences among the groups on two of the three items: the ease of following instructions
provided by the clinician (χ2(3) = 9.14, p = .03) and feeling like a valued partner (χ2(3) =
20.04, p < .001). Post-hoc comparisons using Dunn’s method with a Bonferroni correction
for multiple tests found that the percentage of caregivers in the ODC outcome group who
rated “very true” that the instructions were easy to follow (92%) was significantly lower
than caregivers in the NC group (99%, p < .05), and the percent of caregivers in the ASD
outcome group who rated “very true” to feeling like valued partners (89%) was significantly
lower than caregivers in the other three groups (all ps < .05).
Satisfaction with Tele-Assessment and Diagnostic Feedback: There were
similarly high rates (over 85%) of “very true” ratings across groups on the six statements
related to satisfaction with the tele-assessment process and feedback, but the percentages
of caregivers in the ASD outcome group who rated “very true” to the tele-assessment
meeting their family needs (89%) and understanding the results of the evaluation (86%)
were significantly lower than caregivers in the other groups (97–98%, all ps < .05; see Table
3).
Tele-Assessment and Technology Barriers: Finally, parents were asked if they had
encountered any of the eight barriers listed in Table 3 during the tele-visit. Chi-square tests
indicated small but statistically significant group differences on two items: the caregivers
in the SLC group were significantly more likely to endorse “I didn’t have all the materials
needed” (χ2(1) = 13.46, p = .004) than the caregivers in the NC group, and the ODC group
endorsed “It was hard to play with my child and talk to the clinician at the same time”
(χ2(1) = 9.67, p = .02) significantly more than the NC group. There were very low rates of
endorsement (under 10%) on the other six items related to technology and tele-assessment
barriers, with no differences among the four outcome groups.

social communication and speech delays are among the most commonly and accurately
identified areas of developmental concerns within the first years of life by both providers
and parents (Lipkin et al., 2020; Marshall et al., 2020). The ODC group was not included
since the concerns raised within it were heterogeneous (e.g., motor, feeding, sleep,
temperament). The analyses were not relevant to the NC group and its large size would
have inflated concordance estimates, so they were also excluded. Mann-Whitney U tests
were conducted. Rates of satisfaction were high in both groups and there were no significant
differences between the Concordant and Discordant groups on any of the questions (e.g.,
examiner-caregiver partnership, satisfaction with the tele-assessment process and diagnostic

To examine potential disparities related to a “digital divide” within our sample,
we conducted non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H-tests on multiple socio-demographic
characteristics (race, ethnicity, household income, maternal education, and distance from the
medical center). Overall, the results revealed minimal differences in telehealth perception
and satisfaction based on demographic characteristics, with significant differences found
across demographically defined groups on less than 5% of tests conducted (see supplemental
Tables 1–4). The few significant findings were not driven by socio-economic disparities,
with at least half of the significant group differences reported more often in White, Non-
Hispanic groups and across the different income categories.
Discussion
The current investigation is the first to examine caregiver perceptions of direct-to-home tele-
assessments for both children with ASD concerns and children with broader developmental
concerns (e.g., speech-language delays, other developmental concerns such as global
developmental delays, behavioral problems, etc.) in a community-based sample. This sample
is unique in providing insight into the tele-assessment experiences of families who may not
yet be concerned about developmental delays and/or ASD since they enrolled in the study at
an age (i.e., before 10 months) prior to most parent concerns. Additionally, this community-
based sample is broadly representative of caregivers who participate in well-child visits and
can speak to how the implementation of tele-assessment practices may meet the needs of
families whose child is flagged by developmental screening or surveillance (Keehn et al.,
2022; Talbott et al., 2022b; Vanaken et al., 2024).

Overall, the study found high rates of caregiver satisfaction and few concerns with the
tele-procedures and diagnostic feedback delivered remotely. Few caregivers (less than 20%
on most items) endorsed concerns about telehealth prior to the visit and over 80% said they
were not worried or only a little worried about the impending tele-evaluation. After the
tele-assessment evaluation, 85% or more of each outcome group provided ratings of “very
true” for items related to satisfaction with the procedures and feedback.
High rates of satisfaction after the 24-month tele-assessment notwithstanding, there were
some differences in ratings across outcome groups that attained statistical significance,
potentially driven in part by the large size of this sample. While the vast majority
of caregivers across outcome groups rated “very true” to statements about the examiner-
caregiver partnership and satisfaction with the tele-assessment process and diagnostic
feedback, significantly more caregivers in the ASD group endorsed only “somewhat true”
to feeling like valued partners in the tele-assessment process (11%), the evaluation meeting
their needs (11%), and understanding the tele-assessment results (14%) than caregivers in
the other groups (1–5%). This may not be surprising, since when a diagnosis of ASD is
made, there is more information to share, the consequences of the feedback may be more
significant for families, and the next steps toward service provision are more complex.
Nevertheless, it is important to consider these results carefully. For example, a family
navigator, social worker, or another team member could work with families prior to the
appointment and send more detailed information about the activities and materials needed
for the tele-assessment process, provide examples of behavior(s) clinicians will be looking
for, and encourage caregivers to brainstorm potential activities they could introduce during
the tele-assessment to showcase their concerns. Collaborating with families from the outset
will not only make caregivers more explicit partners in the evaluation but will also help
clinicians better tailor the assessment procedures to meet specific family needs. If a family’s
concerns do not seem like they can be adequately addressed in advance, it may signal
the need to route them to a different assessment modality. If families are provided with
means of indicating that they do not understand the results of the evaluation (such as
our post-visit survey) or do not feel that the tele-assessment met their family’s needs, a
second feedback session could be offered to clarify results, answer additional questions, and
provide further resources, a procedure we employed occasionally in this study, particularly
for families who expressed surprise when developmental concerns were raised. Involving
the caregiver as a member of the diagnostic team and working to establish an open, trusting
relationship before, during, and after tele-assessment may impact families’ understanding
of the results and their belief that tele-assessment fully met their needs, both of which
were significantly lower in families whose child received an ASD diagnosis than in families
without developmental concerns.
We also examined differences in tele-assessment perceptions and satisfaction between
two groups of caregivers, those whose outcome did and did not conform with their
pre-assessment beliefs about their child, focusing on families who received diagnostic
feedback consistent with ASD or speech/language delays. Intuitively, it might seem that
families receiving feedback that does not agree with their perceptions of their child may
be the least satisfied with tele-evaluation, but our results indicated high rates of satisfaction
with the examiner-caregiver partnership, the tele-assessment process, and the diagnostic

Previous research has indicated significant disparities in access to diagnostic assessments
and services among low-resourced communities (Durkin et al., 2015; Hine et al., 2020;
Zwaigenbaum et al., 2021). Telehealth may be a useful medium for closing some of
these gaps; however, it is critical to ensure that already disadvantaged communities do
not experience further disparities in using telehealth diagnostic platforms due to the so-
called “digital divide” (i.e., not having adequate or consistent digital access; Zwaigenbaum
et al., 2021). As more direct-to-home tele-assessment practices emerge, it is important
to track and understand if these practices further disadvantage certain groups based on
socio-demographic characteristics. This study found minimal effects on perspectives and
satisfaction with the tele-assessment process based on race, ethnicity, maternal education
level, household income, and distance from the medical center, supporting the utility of
telehealth for many families and broader community needs.
There are several limitations to the current study to acknowledge. First, we were unable
to capture the responses of all the families enrolled due to the implementation of the
pre- and post-visit caregiver perspectives questionnaires halfway through the study. The
questionnaires were constrained by the use of forced-choice rating systems and did not
include more open-ended response options, which may have revealed other perspectives.
Finally, we examined caregivers’ perspectives immediately before and after their 24-
month tele-assessment evaluation. A more longitudinal approach for capturing concerns,
satisfaction, and perspectives over a longer timeframe might highlight more nuanced
perceptions of direct-to-home tele-assessment practices. Our ability to examine effects of
a potential “digital divide” were limited by the inclusion criteria for the study. While there
were many families located far from the medical center and from lower socio-economic
circumstances, they all had the technology required for participation in this study, by
default. Despite these limitations, the study has many strengths, including being the one
of the largest samples of children with developmental concerns other than ASD to examine
telehealth satisfaction and perceptions. This is also the first study to look at perceptions
and satisfaction for direct-to-home tele-assessments, both pre- and post-visit. We did not
find that families who lived far from the medical center or had lower household income
or maternal education experienced more technology barriers. Finally, the study captures
the perspectives of a large group of caregivers who do not have prior concerns about the
developmental functioning of their children, which may provide insight into the future use of
telehealth platforms for community screening and surveillance.
In conclusion, these findings highlight the feasibility and acceptability of telehealth
platforms to provide assessments and facilitate conversations with experts (Vanaken et al.,
2024), which may accelerate the pathway to intervention for children with developmental
concerns that include and extend beyond ASD. As long as treatment services remain
