Abstract
Background: Infants with prematurity, low birthweight, and medical comorbidities are at high
risk for developmental delays and neurodevelopmental disabilities and require close monitoring.
Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, high-risk infant follow-up (HRIF) programs have adapted to
perform developmental assessments via telehealth.
Objectives: Describe the referral rates to initiate, continue, or increase/add early intervention
(EI) therapies based on in-person use of the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development,
4th Edition (BSID-IV) or telehealth use of the Developmental Assessment in Young Children, 2nd
Edition (DAYC-2).

at an academic medical center in Southern California. Patients were divided into in-person (BSID-

1. Introduction
Children with a history of prematurity, low birthweight, or medical comorbidities during
the perinatal period are known to have a higher risk of developmental delays and
neurodevelopmental disabilities (NDD) in infancy and toddlerhood [1–3]. High-risk infant
follow-up (HRIF) programs are dedicated to closely monitoring at-risk neonates, infants,
and toddlers for developmental delays and providing referrals to early intervention (EI)
therapies, as needed [1]. Receipt of EI therapies in early childhood promotes increased
skill acquisition during a critical period of development and is associated with improved
cognitive, motor, language/communication, social-emotional, and adaptive behavioral
outcomes [1–6]. HRIF programs, which offer clinical care in the immediate years following
discharge from the NICU, seek to coordinate timely connection to services, as well as
assist with complex care needs in order to improve both short- and long-term outcomes [1–
3,7,8]. Many states have their own HRIF program guidelines that detail qualifying medical
and non-medical criteria for clinical follow-up [9]. Each program generally designates
the number and frequency of clinical follow-up visits, which are usually scheduled at
progressive timepoints based on the patient’s age [1]. These visits can span from hospital
discharge to an adjusted age of three years. In California, a Title V program called the
California Children’s Services (CCS) sets the medical eligibility criteria for HRIF programs
within the state [1]. CCS medical eligibility criteria include prematurity (gestational age <32
weeks), low birthweight (birthweight ≤1500 g), and other known risk factors for NDD, such
as congenital heart disease, pulmonary conditions requiring prolonged use of supplemental
oxygen, central nervous system abnormalities (e.g., hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy,
neonatal seizures, high-grade intraventricular hemorrhage), and systemic infections in the
perinatal period [1,3] (see Appendix 1 for complete list of criteria).
To identify delays in early childhood, the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development,
4th Edition (BSID-IV) was released in 2019 and is one of the most common assessment

administered to children from 16 days old to 42 months old and consists of cognitive,
language (expressive and receptive), motor (fine and gross), social-emotional, and adaptive
behavior domain scales, which each provide their respective composite scores, percentile
ranks, and developmental age equivalents [18]. Three areas of development—cognitive,
language, and motor—are directly assessed by an examiner using multiple manipulable
objects in the BSID-IV assessment kit, while social-emotional and adaptive behaviors are
evaluated based on caregiver report [17,19]. Of note, the BSID-IV corrects for patient
prematurity by age adjusting up to two years.
During the COVID-19 pandemic, many HRIF programs recognized the need to conduct
clinical visits through telehealth and adapted developmental assessments for telehealth
platforms [20,21]. Although the BSID-IV can be implemented for in-person visits, it cannot
be utilized in a telehealth context due to specific assessment requirements and the use
of testing objects during its administration [22]. In September 2020, the HRIF program
at a tertiary care medical center in Southern California introduced the Developmental
Assessment of Young Children, 2nd Edition (DAYC-2) into its clinical flow as an alternative
developmental assessment tool for telehealth visits.
The DAYC-2, released in 2012, is a normed and validated diagnostic instrument for
identifying developmental delays in children from birth until the age of 5 years and
11 months [23–25]. The DAYC-2 consists of five subtests: cognition, communication,
social-emotional development, physical development, and adaptive behavior [23]. Similar
to the BSID-IV, a complete DAYC-2 provides discrete standard scores for five different
domains: cognition, receptive language, expressive language, gross motor, and fine motor.
These scores correlate with percentile ranks and age equivalents [26]. Unlike the BSID-
IV, the DAYC-2 generates developmental scores based on a patient’s chronological age,
rather than adjusted age, at the time of assessment. Qualified examiners include early
childhood specialists, psychologists, speech-language pathologists, physical therapists, and
occupational therapists. Using the DAYC-2, examiners collect information through direct
observation, caregiver report, and direct assessment; however, compared to the BSID-IV, the
DAYC-2 relies more heavily on caregiver report for all subtests and can be completed via
telehealth [20].
A primary goal of both the BSID-IV and the DAYC-2 is to identify children who are
developmentally delayed compared to their peers. The use of these assessments serves
as a standardized way to monitor development over time and is helpful for determining
a child’s need for early developmental services. The DAYC-2 has been validated for
measuring early childhood development based on criterion-prediction validation data against
the Battelle Developmental Inventory, 2nd Edition and the Developmental Observation
Checklist System, 2nd Edition. The 3rd edition of the BSID was used as a point of reference
for designing the format and content of the DAYC-2; however, the DAYC-2 has not yet
been validated against any edition of the BSID [26]. To date, there have been no studies
analyzing parallel use of the BSID-IV and the DAYC-2 or the resulting referral rates for
EI therapies following administration of either assessment in the high-risk infant population

2. Methods
2.1. Study methodology
We received Institutional Review Board approval from a tertiary care medical center prior to
starting the study. This study was a retrospective chart review of 203 patients who received
care in the HRIF program at the tertiary care medical center. The inclusion criteria for
this study were infants and toddlers who (1) received clinical care in the HRIF program
from March 1, 2020 to March 1, 2021, and (2) received a complete BSID-IV or DAYC-2
assessment during their clinical visit. The clinic first introduced the DAYC-2 assessment for
telehealth visits on September 3, 2020; therefore, the start and end dates of the chart review
timeline—March 1, 2020 and March 1, 2021, respectively—were selected as markers for 6
months pre- and 6 months post-introduction of the DAYC-2.
As part of the HRIF program’s guidelines, developmental assessments were scheduled at
three different timepoints: when patients reached the chronological age of 6 months (“Visit
1”), 12 months (“Visit 2”), and 24 months (“Visit 3”). All patients seen in-person received
the BSID-IV, and all patients seen via telehealth received the DAYC-2. In accordance
with the design and scoring protocols of these two assessments, all BSID-IV assessments
were scored based on adjusted age, whereas all DAYC-2 assessments were scored based
on chronological age. Our study was originally designed to capture assessment data from
a single timepoint within the one-year study period, rather than optimize for longitudinal
data analysis. To ensure uniformity in our approach, we included the assessment type
and scores from chronologically earlier visits and excluded those from later visits for
patients who received more than one developmental assessment within the one-year study
period. All developmental assessments were administered by either a physical therapist or
occupational therapist in the clinic. At the end of each visit, a pediatric physician interpreted
the developmental scores and provided a clinical determination regarding the need for EI
therapies. A conceptual model (Fig. B.1 in Appendix B) is included.
The three physical therapists and four occupational therapists who administered the
assessments included in this analysis were already familiar with administering the 1st
through 3rd editions of the BSID. In January 2020, all physical and occupational therapists
received an intensive in-person training on how to administer the BSID-IV from a
representative of Pearson Assessments. Select therapists elected to become research reliable
by pursuing BSID-IV certification, a process that involves additional training sessions with
expert clinicians and submission of their video recordings to determine inter-rater reliability.
However, not all therapists whose assessment data were included in this study sought
BSID-IV certification. Prior to September 2020, the lead occupational therapist in the HRIF

2.2. Information from medical documentation
Information gathered from the electronic medical record review included demographic
details (chronological age at visit for full-term patients, adjusted age at visit for preterm
patients, gender, ethnicity, race, and health insurance type), medical information (gestational
age if born preterm, category of prematurity if born preterm, primary medical diagnosis,
and current status of EI therapies), developmental scores based on the BSID-IV or the
DAYC-2, and clinical recommendations regarding EI therapies. Of note, ethnicity and race
information was entered into the medical record by hospital nursing staff upon initial
admission and was not provided by caregivers during the clinical visit as self-identified
information. At the end of each visit, the pediatric physician stated whether a patient (1)
does not need EI therapies, (2) should initiate EI therapies, (3) should continue their current
EI therapies as-is, or (4) should increase the amount of their current EI therapies or add a
new type of EI therapy.
2.3. Statistical analyses
We stratified the sample based on type of visit and the corresponding developmental
assessment completed: telehealth (DAYC-2) or in-person (BSID-IV). We characterized each
developmental domain of the DAYC-2 and BSID-IV assessments in accordance with the
age-standardized score range interpretations from their respective scoring manuals. The
BSID-IV composite scores fell within the following categories: very poor (<55), poor (55–
70), below average (71–85), average (86–115), above average (116–130), and superior (131–
145). The DAYC-2 standard scores fell within the following categories: very poor (<70),
poor (70–79), below average (80–89), average (90–110), above average (111–120), and
superior (121–130). We examined differences between the two assessment groups in terms
of demographic characteristics, medical information, and clinical recommendations using
chi-square (χ2) or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U test
for continuous variables. Further subgroup comparisons were performed using the subsets
of patients who were referred and not referred for EI therapies at the time of their visit. All
analyses were conducted using Stata/MP version-17.0 [28].

4. Discussion
4.1. Key findings
There was no evidence of a significant difference in the rates of referral to initiate EI
therapies between patients in the telehealth (DAYC-2) and in-person (BSID-IV) groups. The
telehealth group patients already receiving therapies tended to have higher rates of referral to
increase/add EI therapies compared to in-person group patients. The telehealth and in-person
groups in this study were demographically and clinically comparable.
Further subgroup analyses of the patient subsets, grouped by current status of EI therapies
and clinical recommendation, did reveal some differences. Telehealth patients who were
already receiving EI therapies but not recommended to increase/add EI therapies were
generally older than their counterparts in the in-person group. The same subgroup of
telehealth patients were also more likely to have public health insurance compared to the
in-person subgroup. In the telehealth group, patients who were not receiving EI therapies
and not referred to initiate them were more likely to have private health insurance than
their counterparts in the in-person group. Premature telehealth patients who were already
receiving therapies and referred to increase/add EI therapies were born more preterm (a
difference of 4 weeks in median gestational age) than their counterparts in the in-person

a referral for EI therapies, and a developmental score provides one piece of supporting
information among many [29–31].
One important difference between the BSID-IV and the DAYC-2 is the lack of correction
for prematurity in the DAYC-2, which results in developmental scores based on a patient’s
chronological age. This lack of age adjustment must be accounted for in the clinician’s
interpretation of DAYC-2 scores and factored into the clinical recommendation for EI
therapies. Providers in our HRIF program anecdotally reported incorporating their own
clinical expertise and understanding of age-appropriate developmental milestones when
interpreting the DAYC-2 scores; they compared DAYC-2 age equivalents with the adjusted
age, rather than chronological age, for patients born preterm. The DAYC-2’s lack of
correction for prematurity also likely explains the right-skewed, or lower, overall distribution
of developmental scores in the telehealth group. However, it is difficult to draw any
conclusions regarding EI referral rates based on DAYC-2 scores alone, as providers took
patient prematurity into account prior to making an EI referral. From our perspective,
accounting for prematurity when referencing the DAYC-2 norm-referenced scores is
preferred, as this is consistent with standard practice in developmental assessments [32].
The scheduling of in-person and telehealth visits over the course of our study
period reflected realistic considerations of families and real-time clinical adaptations to
administering developmental assessments during the COVID-19 pandemic. Our results
suggest that an alternative tool to the BSID-IV, such as the DAYC-2, can be administered via
telehealth and lead to similar EI referral rates.
4.2. Limitations
One limitation of this study is the lack of direct validation data between the DAYC-2 and
the BSID-IV; with this in mind, we did not intend to carry out a validation study. Our study
more closely resembles a feasibility study, with the DAYC-2 being used in parallel with the
BSID-IV during a time of decreased accessibility to in-person developmental assessment.
Another limitation is the lack of simultaneous administration of both the DAYC-2 and the
BSID-IV for each patient, which would have allowed for a comparison of developmental
scores and EI referral rates at one clinical timepoint. Although this study includes
a comparison of demographic characteristics and primary medical diagnoses for both
assessment groups, we cannot infer that the composition of patients referred to initiate
or increase/add EI therapies in the telehealth group matched the composition of the
corresponding in-person group exactly. Although referral rates for initiating EI therapies
were not significantly different following use of the DAYC-2 or the BSID-IV, our data does
not provide information regarding whether each assessment would have identified the same
children.

Multiple examiners (a total of three physical therapists and four occupational therapists)
were individually engaged in the administration and scoring of both in-person and telehealth
assessments. All therapists received standardized training on how to administer the BSID-
IV; however, not all therapists received BSID-IV certification to establish research reliability
prior to the start of the study period, due to the retrospective nature of this study. Within
this group of therapists, there may have been inter-rater variability for multiple measures,
including the scoring of patient observations and interpretation of caregiver responses.
Interpretation of the DAYC-2 scores for premature patients relied heavily on provider
expertise and clinical judgement due to the lack of correction for prematurity in the design
of this assessment. Two pediatric physicians were individually involved in interpreting
developmental outcome scores and providing EI-related recommendations, a process that
may entail variability in clinical judgement.
In this study, we lacked information regarding factors influencing a caregiver’s decision
when presented with the choice between an in-person or telehealth visit. In a recent
study, factors prompting a preference for telehealth visits over in-person visits were
outlined, including reduced risk of viral transmission, complex healthcare needs (e.g.,
reliance on gastrotomy and/or tracheostomy tubes), considerable geographical distance
from the clinic, transportation-related barriers, conflicting work schedules, and caregiver
health complications or disabilities [27]. We acknowledge that these additional pieces
of information, not collected in this study, likely influenced caregiver preference for an
in-person or telehealth visit.
Additional considerations include language-related differences for non-English speaking
caregivers and differing levels of health literacy among caregivers. This study did not collect
data regarding the primary languages of caregivers or the use of language interpretation
during clinical visits. The use of a professional language interpreter via phone, video, or
in-person interpretation may in itself have had effects on the delivery and interpretation of
developmental assessments [33–36].
Lastly, we calculated BSID-IV scores utilizing the standardized scores released in 2019 [18].
Although new normative data for the BSID-IV were published in July 2023, we considered
it inappropriate to alter the scores, given that clinician interpretation of scores were based
on the normed scores from 2019. Consequently, select BSID-IV scores in the average and
above average ranges may be lower than intended.
4.3. Clinical implications
Key benefits of telehealth visits include a reduction in risk of viral transmission, decreased
financial and time costs associated with transportation, and an opportunity to observe a
patient’s natural home environment [20,27,37,38]. The adaptability of the DAYC-2 for
telehealth increases access to childhood developmental assessment in remote and rural
communities and during times of public health emergencies. A significant factor to consider
when conducting telehealth visits is the necessity for patients and caregivers to have a
personal electronic device, such as a computer, tablet, or smartphone, with a reliable internet
connection. Hence, access to technology may continue to be a barrier contributing to
healthcare disparities. With the emergence of telehealth developmental assessments, it is

If use of the DAYC-2 was found to be associated with insufficient referrals for EI therapies

encouraged when the opportunity for an in-person assessment is possible. When in-person
assessment is inaccessible, HRIF programs may consider telehealth administration of an
alternative tool, such as the DAYC-2. A better understanding of how to use the DAYC-2 in
a variety of clinical contexts can promote clinician comfort with administering this type of
assessment and translating assessment scores into clinical recommendations.
4.4. Future research
A key next step will be to investigate whether the DAYC-2 and BSID-IV are identifying
development delays and leading to EI referrals at similar rates within the same cohort of
individuals in an HRIF program. This type of direct comparison would better inform how
these two developmental assessments can be utilized in HRIF programs and potentially
highlight whether one assessment may be preferable or optimal over the other in in-person
and telehealth contexts. The likelihood of placing an EI referral may also vary depending on
an individual’s age at the time of assessment; to limit the effects of this potential confounder,
future studies should include analyses of EI referral rates at isolated timepoints or with
patient groups separated by age.
Our patient cohort was observed to include families with different primary languages
and varying degrees of health literacy, although this information was not collected
systematically. Further research is needed to determine how language-related factors and
the use of audio/video versus in-person language interpretation during developmental
assessments may potentially affect clinical outcomes [35,39]. Additionally, social
determinants of health—including economic stability, educational background, access to
resources, neighborhood, and social supports—were not included in our study. These factors
have been shown to influence access to healthcare and developmental services, including EI
therapies, and should be a focal point in future studies [40,41].
5. Conclusion
The BSID-IV is commonly used for capturing developmental delays in the high-risk infant
population due to its excellent validity, updated normative data, use of direct observation,
and age adjustment for prematurity. However, medical, financial, transportation-related,
and/or logistic barriers may limit the ability of HRIF programs to consistently offer
and administer the BSID-IV in an in-person setting. An alternative instrument, such as
