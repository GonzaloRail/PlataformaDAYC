The quality of parent-child interactions at an early age has been linked to children’s social-emotional development, executive
function, and risk for behavior problems. As such, parent-child interactions in naturalistic settings could present a unique
opportunity to screen for at-risk behavior in young children, enabling timely and targeted interventions. In this work, we
validate the feasibility of using structured at-home play sessions, completed via the Tandem smartphone app, to enable highly
accurate and scalable behavioral assessments. We demonstrate that audio and physiological signals recorded during the play
session can be used to capture key markers of parent-child interaction dynamics, which are more indicative of at-risk behavior
compared to features from each individual alone. We propose novel audio-based dyadic interaction features that significantly
outperform conventional speech features at predicting risk for behavior problems, achieving an F1 score of 0.87. Furthermore,
we show that dyadic physiological synchrony features, extracted from privacy-preserving wearable sensor data, can classify
at-risk behavior with an F1 score of 0.91. Tandem thus sets the stage for automated at-home behavior assessment tools for
young children that balance screening accuracy with practical deployment considerations.

INTRODUCTION

As humans, interacting with caregivers during early childhood is one of our most formative interpersonal
experiences. Parenting practices and parent-child interactions at an early age are key drivers of children’s
verbal development and language acquisition [37, 78], academic readiness [67], executive function [80], and
social-emotional development [48, 85]. As a result, there has been sustained and widespread interest in observing,
quantifying, and improving early childhood parent-child interactions both within the child development [22, 32]
and ubiquitous computing (UbiComp)/human-computer interaction (HCI) research communities [40, 42, 44, 45].
A large body of work in child development has especially focused on the association between parent-child
interaction quality and behavior problems in young children. Communication patterns within the parent-child

accessible at-home screening tools that respect family privacy while providing valuable clinical insights. Future
research in the UbiComp and HCI communities can build on these principles to develop and deploy scalable tools
for at-home screening that balance assessment accuracy with practical deployment considerations.

2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Parent-Child Interaction Quality and Behavior Problems
The quality of children’s interactions with their primary caregivers at a young age plays a critical role in the
development of chronic self-regulation difficulties and subsequent behavior problems [24, 65]. Parent-child
interaction synchrony has been shown to promote the development of self-regulation and adjustment strategies,
while dysynchrony, emotional distress, or negative tone in these interactions increases children’s risk for poor
self-regulation and emotion socialization [23, 81, 83]. In recent years, research has shown that parent and child
speech acts measured using the Dyadic Parent-Child Interaction Coding System (DPICS; [22]) are associated with
internalizing and externalizing problems [6, 16, 33]. Zlomke et al. found significant differences in parent-child
interaction behavior observed via DPICS codes across children with and without symptoms of autism spectrum
disorder [95]. Keown found that paternal and maternal sensitivity, warmth, positive regard, and intrusive behavior
were differentially associated with children’s ADHD symptoms at preschool age and through middle childhood
[49]. A dynamic systems analysis of parent-child interaction behavior found that behavioral rigidity, defined in
terms of the durations of and transitions between positive, negative, and neutral parent and child behaviors, was
associated with concurrent internalizing and externalizing behaviors as well as their progression over time [38].
Prior work has also found associations between parent mental health and children’s current externalizing and
internalizing problems [7] as well as future development of conduct problems [15], which were further mediated
by the quality of the home environment and of parent-child interactions. In addition to the transactional processes

depression, dysthymia, and various anxiety disorders (separation anxiety disorder, generalized anxiety disorder,
etc). In this work, we attempt to enable similar broadband screening for early childhood behavior problems using
automatically extracted measures of parent-child interaction, thus reducing the reliance on subjectively reported
measures for clinical diagnosis.

2.2

Physiological Correlates of Behavior Problems

The regulation of physiological arousal, especially in challenging task contexts, has been theorized to be a key
component of children’s self-regulation abilities that develop in early childhood, the lack of which can manifest
as behavioral and emotional disorders [8, 17, 64]. Prior research has established links between parasympathetic
nervous system activity (indexed by heart rate variability or HRV) and the regulation of emotions, attention,
and behavior [9, 72]. Respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), or the variability in heart rate within the respiratory
frequencies, has also been associated with emotional reactivity [86]. Suppression of RSA in challenging contexts
is further indicative of active and appropriate coping [73], while RSA dysregulation has been associated with
ADHD, disruptive behavior disorders, and other behavior problems [17, 87, 89]. Similarly, researchers have also
studied the relationship between behavior problems and sympathetic nervous system activity, often indexed
by electrodermal activity (EDA). Morris et al. found that ADHD was associated with higher RSA and elevated
EDA reactivity during an emotion suppression task [64]. Baker et al. found that low EDA levels during child
compliance tasks predicted higher externalizing behavior [3]. In the context of parent-child interactions, Moffitt
et al. found a link between more supportive parent reactions and scaffolding and higher baseline RSA and higher
electrodermal reactivity in children on the autism spectrum [63]. Parental scaffolding during problem-solving
tasks also moderated the relationship between EDA under-arousal and externalizing problems [3]. In light of these
findings, our study recorded HR, HRV, RSA, and EDA signals from the parent and child in a dyadic interaction
setting using wrist-worn wearables. We used these signals to obtain measures of parasympathetic and sympathetic
autonomous nervous system reactivity that were in turn leveraged to predict risk for behavior problems.
In addition to individual physiological reactivity, recent work has shown that physiological synchrony, or
the alignment between the physiological responses of the parent and child in a dyadic setting, has important
implications for parent-child relationships and child behavior. Parent-child dyadic RSA synchrony has been shown
to intensify the effect of parenting behaviors on youth behavior problems [66], and lower RSA synchrony during
mild stress and free play has been linked to higher risk for internalizing and externalizing problems [59]. Lower
parent-child EDA synchrony during free play has been associated with higher child autism spectrum disorder
symptoms [4]. Our work further examines whether measures of heart rate, RSA, and EDA synchrony between
parent and child during a structured play session can be utilized to screen children for behavior problems.

Parent-Child Interactions and Behavior Assessment in UbiComp Settings

There has been significant interest within the UbiComp and HCI community in observing and improving parentchild interactions in different settings [82]. TalkBetter [42] and TalkLIME [84] record parent-child interactions and
derive metalinguistic features to provide parents feedback on their communication with children. SpecialTime [40]
analyzes parent speech using the Dyadic Parent-Child Interaction Coding System (DPICS) to provide realtime feedback for families undergoing parent-child interaction therapy. Other systems facilitate parent-child
communication during various activities of daily living [12, 44, 51, 93].
In terms of sensing approaches, most prior work reported above has focused on audio-based observations
of parent-child communication. This has led to an investment in the development of datasets and models for
analyzing parent-child speech [40, 45], although many applications still rely on commercially available audio
processing techniques that are not tuned to child speech [92, 93]. A small number of prior studies have also
used wearable sensing to monitor or facilitate parent-child interactions. For example, ParentGuardian used
EDA sensing to monitor parental stress and deliver interventions via smartphone- and tablet-based apps [70].
FamilyScope used HRV and EDA during family interactions to provide feedback about emotional arousal and
stress levels [54]. Chong et al. used wearable cameras to detect children’s gaze and eye contact during interactions
with adults [14].
While there has been considerable interest in behavioral assessments in young children using smartphones
and wearables in home settings (e.g., [2, 46, 57]), there is limited work using parent-child interactions as a setting
for naturalistic behavior assessment. The closest work is by Hernandez et al., who used EDA responses during a
semi-structured play session to detect parent-reported ease of engagement with children [36]. Tandem extends
this field of research by proposing flexible, dual-modal approaches using audio and/or physiological signals to
enable behavioral screening during play-based parent-child interactions.

3 METHODS
3.1 In-home Parent-Child Interaction Study
3.1.1 Participants. To develop Tandem, we recruited 34 preschool-aged children (3- to 5-year-olds) and their
parents (i.e., 68 total participants) to participate in an in-home parent-child interaction study. Parents were
recruited through a developmental science research participant registry at the authors’ institution and via
community outreach and advertisements in the area. Parent-child dyads were eligible to participate as long as
they spoke English as one of the languages at home and the child had not been diagnosed with any developmental
delays or intellectual impairment. Children were not screened for, or selected based on, their history of behavior
problems. 12 of the children were female and 22 were male, with a mean age of 4.3 years (SD: 1 year). The parent
participating in the study was the biological mother (N=27), biological father (N=6), or adoptive mother (N=1)
of the child. The child’s race as reported by the parent was predominantly White (N=30), followed by Black or
African American (N=3) and Asian (N=1), with one child identified as Hispanic or Latino. All study procedures
were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Massachusetts Amherst.
3.1.2 Study Application and Play Session. The parent and child together completed a 25-minute-long, at-home
structured play session using the custom-built Tandem study app (see Figure 1). The app was built to be compatible
with both Android and iOS devices using the Flutter framework [26]. Parents downloaded the app on their
smartphones and reviewed a brief set of instructions before beginning the play session.
We chose to leverage play-based interactions as the basis of our at-home study task as they have long been
established as an important setting for the development of parent-child relationships at a young age [29]. Several
standardized protocols and manual observational measures have been designed to evaluate the quality of parentchild interactions in the context of play [22, 32], with some measures also linked to behavior problems [16].

The play session in our study was based on activities completed by families during Parent-Child Interaction
Therapy (PCIT; [21]). PCIT is an evidence-based treatment for young children with behavior problems, where
the therapist observes parent-child interactions in the clinic while they are engaged in a structured play session
and provides parents with coaching on skills to manage their child’s behavior. We adapted this session for our
study, which consisted of three phases, each of which was initiated by an audio prompt from the app. The session
began with ten minutes of child-led play (CLP), where the instructions asked parents to let their child choose any
activity they want and play with them following their lead. The next ten minutes consisted of parent-led play
(PLP), where the parent was asked to engage the child in playing according to rules set by the parent. Lastly, the
clean-up (CU) phase asked parents to direct the child to put away all toys independently and without assistance.
The CU phase lasted 5 minutes, after which the app informed the parent that the play session was complete.
The app recorded parent and child audio throughout the session. Parents were provided the opportunity to
review their audio data before choosing whether to upload it to a secure server for inclusion in the study. They
were also asked to rate their child’s frustration levels during each phase (CLP, PLP, and CU) on a scale of 1-5.
3.1.3 Audio and Wearable Data. Parent and child audio was recorded via the study app throughout the play
session in order to observe conversation behaviors similar to those that are recorded by PCIT therapists when
observing parent-child interaction in real-time. Two-channel audio was recorded at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz
and a bitrate of 128 Kbps. Physiological signals (heart rate, respiration rate, electrodermal activity, and skin
temperature) were recorded using an Empatica EmbracePlus wristband worn by the parent and child on their
non-dominant wrist. Parents were asked to ensure that both themselves and their child put on their respective
wristbands at least five minutes before starting the play session in order to record baseline physiological data and
for children to get used to the device.

3.2

Audio Processing and Feature Extraction

Figure 2 shows the audio processing pipeline comprising automatic speech recognition, time alignment, speaker
diarization, and feature extraction. The following sections describe each of these steps in more detail.
3.2.1 Transcription and Speaker Diarization. The parent-child interaction audio was first automatically transcribed and diarized in preparation for feature extraction. We utilized child audio and speech benchmark models
from the recent work on Playlogue [45] to process dyadic audio, since our participant age range is similar to
that of this prior work. Audio recordings were downsampled from 44.1 kHz to 16 kHz. Audio from each play
session is trimmed to form three separate clips, one for each phase of the session (CLP, PLP, or CU). The audio
instructions from the app at the start of each phase were removed from the final clips, which contained only
parent and child audio.
The OpenAI Whisper-large-v3 model [75], fine-tuned on the Playlogue dataset [45], was used to automatically
transcribe parent-child audio recordings. Since Whisper does not provide accurate timestamps per segment
(Whisper’s word-level timestamps include silences between words/segments), the output transcript was forcedaligned to the audio using the NeMo Forced Aligner tool from the NVIDIA NeMo Framework [35].
To classify adult vs. child speech, the pre-trained WavLM-large model [13] was first used to extract per-frame
speech embeddings. A multilayer perceptron model with two hidden layers was trained on the Playlogue dataset
using WavLM features to predict speaker identities for each audio frame (parent vs. child). The model was then
used to infer per-frame speaker identities for the parent-child interaction audio in the present study.
To further ensure accurate diarization, we implemented a novel ASR-aware diarization correction procedure
that used the segment start and end timestamps from the aligned ASR transcripts. First, the per-frame speaker
predictions from the diarization model were extracted for each frame within the aligned ASR segment. Since
each segment represents a single sentence that is likely spoken by the same individual, the majority speaker label
was assigned to the entire segment. The correction procedure was evaluated on the Playlogue test set, leading to
a significant reduction in the overall diarization error rate (DER) from the 31.8% reported in [45] to 24.6%.
We then evaluated this modified pipeline on audio clips from three participants (∼ 10%) in the present study
that were manually transcribed and assigned ground-truth speaker labels. The pipeline achieved a DER of 30.1%
and an ASR word error rate of 11.8% on these clips, demonstrating performance that is comparable or significantly
better than that on the Playlogue test set as reported in [45]. The automated pipeline was also found to preserve
most of the semantic content compared to ground truth, with a BERTScore [94] F1 of 0.97. Given the strong
performance of the pipeline on the three validation clips, we used it to automatically transcribe and diarize
segments from the rest of the data in preparation for downstream audio feature extraction.
3.2.2 Conversational Features. We first extracted “conversational features” from the transcribed parent-child
audio that have previously been linked to child development and behavior to evaluate whether they would
be predictive of behavior problems. The aligned start and end timestamps and speaker labels were used to
compute the duration of parent and child speech over the entire session and within each phase. The diarization
information was used to calculate the number of conversation turns, defined as a back-and-forth alternation
between the parent and child (or vice versa) with no more than five seconds between them, which are associated
with children’s social-emotional development [30]. Finally, the mean length of utterance (MLU), a measure of
children’s gross language development [68], was calculated in terms of the mean number of words per utterance.

parent-child or child-parent transitions (32 + 32 = 64 transitions). This means that the former intra-individual
transitions are over-represented in the feature set compared to inter-individual transitions. However, the DPICS
interaction features represent the frequency of each of these transitions – a higher number of intra-individual
transitions indicates that the parent and child demonstrate less turn-taking behavior, which has been linked
to developmental delays and behavior problems [19]. Similarly, a higher frequency of Child Negative Talk in
response to Parent Command can indicate non-compliance and oppositional behavior. The DPICS interaction
features capture these various measures in a single representation of dyadic behavior. Additionally, while these
transition-based features still have some temporal locality by being restricted to sequences of only two immediate
sentences, the overall frequency distribution of these transitions spanning a session provides valuable insight
into both parent-child turn-taking behavior as well as both parties’ immediate reactions to natural breakpoints in
the conversation.

Wearable Data and Physiological Feature Extraction

3.3.1 Signal Preprocessing. Based on prior literature (see Section 2.2), heart rate and electrodermal activity (EDA)
signals recorded via the EmbracePlus wristband were used to extract physiological features that could indicate
risk for behavior problems. The systolic peak time series from the EmbracePlus was cleaned using the artifact
correction algorithm proposed by Lipponen and Tarvainen [56]. EDA signals were cleaned using a low-pass
filter with a cutoff of 1 Hz [74]. EDA artifacts were removed by discarding all 5-second segments where the
signal changes by over 0.1 𝜇𝑆 in less than 1 second [28]. Missing EDA segments that were less than 15 s long
were interpolated using monotonic cubic interpolation, while longer chunks of missing data were discarded. The
cleaned signals were used to extract individual physiological features from the parent and child as well as dyadic
physiological synchrony features, as described in the following sections.

3.3.2 Individual Physiological Features. The cleaned systolic peak signal from each participant was used to
extract heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), and respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) features from the
entire play session as well as for each phase of the session (where phase-level features were computed based
on the signals recorded between the start and end times of each phase). Mean HR was extracted from peak
differences after discarding consecutive peaks that were closer than 400 ms or farther than 1200 ms in order to
exclude implausible heart periods based on normal human ranges[25, 61]. HRV was computed in terms of the
root mean square of successive differences between peaks. Following recommendations from prior work [55],
RSA was calculated using the Porges–Bohrer method [71] as the natural logarithm of the RSA signal variances
over 30-second epochs. 4 out of 68 participants had inadequate length of filtered signals to extract phase-level
HR, HR, and EDA features during at least one phase of the play session. However, session-level features were
extracted successfully from all participants’ data.
The cleaned EDA signal was decomposed into tonic and phasic components using the convex optimization
approach proposed by Greco et al. [31]. The tonic component was used to extract the mean skin conductance level
(SCL). The phasic component was used to extract skin conductance response (SCR) peaks using the algorithm
proposed by Kim et al. [50]. The number of SCR peaks and the mean SCR peak amplitude were calculated for the
whole session and for each phase. Due to signal quality issues, phase-level EDA features were missing for at least
one of the three phases for 10 out of the 68 participants. Session-level EDA features were successfully extracted
from all participants.
The above HR/HRV and EDA features together form the “individual physiological features” that were used to
train models to detect behavior problems.
3.3.3 Physiological Synchrony Features. In addition to individual physiological features extracted from the
systolic peak and electrodermal activity signals recorded by the parent and child’s EmbracePlus devices, we
computed measures of dyadic “physiological synchrony” that have been shown to indicate risk for behavior
problems in prior work.
Following prior work, we computed the inter-beat interval (IBI) synchrony as the cross-correlation between
parent and child IBI [1, 11, 34]. The individual IBI peak signals were filtered to remove intervals outside of the
normal range of 0.4 to 1.2 seconds, aligned across participants, and interpolated and resampled to 1 Hz. We then fit
an autoregressive integrated moving average model to eliminate autocorrelations and use the residuals to compute
cross-correlation at a lag of 0. Additionally, to capture alignment between parent and child IBI at different time
lags, we computed the distance between the normalized IBI series using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) [27, 79].
To quantify dyadic RSA synchrony, we computed the Pearson correlation between time-aligned RSA signals
from each participant, similar to other linear modeling approaches used in prior work on parent-child dyads
[5, 66]. Finally, we estimated EDA synchrony by calculating the Pearson correlation between the time-aligned
parent and child signals [4, 36]. We also computed the DTW distance between the normalized EDA signals from
the parent and child [27, 36]. Physiological synchrony features were computed separately for each phase of the
play session as well as for the overall session. 5 out of the 34 dyadic pairs had missing physiological synchrony
features due to poor signal quality for either the child’s or parent’s wearable signals.

selection was performed via pruning with a prune ratio of 0.5 (i.e., pruning up to half of the original features at
each step). Missing features were passed on to base models to be handled on a per-model basis.

Multimodal Audio+Physiology Models for Behavior Assessment

The analyses presented in the preceding sections demonstrate that at-home dyadic play sessions provide a
valuable setting for automated behavior assessment, with both audio and physiological signals independently
yielding highly informative features that are predictive of at-risk behavior. This enables child mental health
researchers and practitioners to choose either modality for evaluation based on factors such as classification
accuracy, ease of deployment, participant comfort, or privacy considerations.
While we observe that physiological features achieve classification performance that is comparable to or
better than that of audio features, we hypothesize that the two observational modalities contain complementary
information about the dyadic processes at play during parent-child interaction. Therefore, we evaluate whether
it is possible to leverage both sets of features to train multimodal models for behavior assessment that are more
performant than the audio- or physiology-based models presented previously. To incorporate audio information
in these models, we use the DPICS interaction features that showed the best classification performance among
audio-based classification models in Section 4.1. However, unlike dyadic audio features that are extracted from
a single stream of audio recording on the phone, physiological signals are recorded via separate devices worn
by the parent and child. Obtaining physiological features from both parent and child therefore necessitates the
deployment of two devices, which can be expensive and challenging in real-world situations. In light of these
considerations, we evaluate multimodal models with different physiology-based feature sets, viz., child physiology
features only, parent physiology features only, individual physiology features from both participants, and dyadic
physiological synchrony features.
Table 3 shows the performance of multimodal risk classification models using the different physiological
feature combinations. We observe that adding individual physiological features from either the child or the parent
does not improve classification performance over the audio-only baseline. In fact, the addition of child physiology
features worsens predictive performance, even as child physiology features achieved an F1 score of 0.86 in
isolation (see Table 2). This counterintuitive result likely reflects that the audio and physiological modalities
capture overlapping aspects of the child’s regulatory state through different mechanisms. More concretely, child
physiological patterns and verbal interaction behaviors may both reflect emotional dysregulation, but may be
prone to collinearity effects that make it difficult for the model to integrate them effectively.
However, we observe that adding individual physiology features from both participants results in more accurate
predictions compared to the audio-only baseline. This suggests that having information on both parent and child
physiological reactivity allows the model to learn complementary dyadic interaction patterns beyond what is
available from verbal features alone. Interestingly, the physiological synchrony features do not have the same
advantageous effect, suggesting that the model may be leveraging different physiological markers from the parent
and child (e.g., EDA vs. HR) rather than the co-regulation of any one response.

interaction patterns exhibited by the parent-child dyad are likely to be unique to each phase. Additionally, in
terms of behavior assessment, we hypothesize that the interaction dynamics from each of the three phases are

4.6

Evaluating the Feasibility of Predicting Multi-dimensional Behavior Symptoms

As described in Section 1, Tandem primarily focuses on behavior assessment by identifying high-level at-risk
behavior that can then be probed further by child mental health practitioners. In this regard, Tandem acts as a
broadband screening mechanism that does not aim to evaluate or score children on specific behavioral dimensions,
instead classifying above vs. below threshold scores on a range of combined scales as discussed in Section 3.1.4.
However, we recognize that researchers and clinicians may be interested in gaining more specific insight
into children’s behavior along different evaluation axes using at-home screening, since this can enable targeted
intervention strategies to address particular issues. To this end, we conduct follow-up analyses investigating the
feasibility of leveraging audio and physiological features from the dyadic play session to predict children’s 𝑇
scores on each of the four BASC-3 standardized composite indices (i.e., “Externalizing Problems”, “Internalizing
Problems”, “Behavioral Symptoms Index”, and “Adaptive Skills”). We used the AutoGluon framework to train

5 DISCUSSION
5.1 Parent-Child Interactions as a Naturalistic yet Controlled Setting for Behavior Assessments
As described previously, a large body of work in the child development and the UbiComp research communities
has hitherto focused on parent-child interactions as an important, but standalone, outcome to measure and
improve [40, 42, 44, 93]. Our work extends this perspective by demonstrating that structured parent-child
interactions can serve a dual purpose, enabling highly accurate behavior assessment based on dyadic interaction
dynamics.

We selected play-based parent-child interactions as the basis for Tandem as they provide an ideal balance
between controlled assessment (such as in clinical settings) and ecological validity. The structured nature of the
interaction allows us to establish a more comparable setup across participants when studying child mental health
in naturalistic environments. Furthermore, the play session is based on clinically validated observational settings
used in parent-child interaction therapy [22], and prior research links interaction behaviors captured in this
setting to various developmental outcomes [16].
Our findings demonstrate that interaction dynamics during this structured play session are indeed valuable for
predicting behavior problems. The clean-up phase proved particularly revealing of at-risk behavior when using
audio-based DPICS interaction features, achieving performance matching that of the entire session with an F1
score of 0.87. This suggests that specific task structures can elicit diagnostically valuable behavioral patterns,
which aligns with clinical practices in parent-child interaction therapy [21] while making them accessible outside
formal settings. The findings open new directions for exploring shorter, more targeted assessment protocols that
could be easily integrated into home routines while maintaining clinical validity.
From a practical standpoint, parent-child interactions have several characteristics that make them an ideal
setting for behavioral assessments. First, leveraging day-to-day interactions in the home makes behavioral
screenings much more accessible and scalable compared to clinical visits. The ability to collect relevant information
about interaction dynamics using commercial, off-the-shelf devices such as smartphones and wearables further
facilitates convenient and scalable assessments. Using play-based interactions, in particular, also makes the
screening process feel more natural to the child [46]. Lastly, as shown in Section 2, there is strong evidence
linking parent-child interaction patterns to behavior problems. Utilizing simple, interpretable features that are
known to be associated with behavioral outcomes allows researchers to develop explainable predictive models as
well as design targeted interventions to then improve behavioral outcomes. These findings highlight the potential
for leveraging parent-child interaction behavior as a promising approach for in-the-wild assessments of child
behavior problems.

5.2

Physiological Signals as an Alternative to Audio-based Interaction Analysis

While audio sensing has dominated parent-child interaction research in UbiComp settings [40, 42, 84], our
findings show that physiological signals provide more informative features and stronger predictive performance
for behavior assessment in young children (F1 score of 0.87 for audio vs. 0.91 for physiology). Our physiological
sensing approach successfully detected dyadic patterns associated with at-risk behavior through measures of
autonomic nervous system activity, aligning with prior research showing associations between physiological
reactivity and behavior problems [8].
This represents a significant paradigm shift for measuring parent-child interaction dynamics and their association with behavior problems. Physiological data can be collected continuously and unobtrusively through
commercially available smartwatches and wearable devices. It offers a privacy-preserving alternative that eliminates the need to record potentially sensitive audio content. Wearable-based assessment is also a compelling
alternative in multilingual households or when parent-child interactions may be interrupted by other family
members, where audio processing presents additional challenges.
The strong performance of child-only physiological features (F1 score of 0.86) is also particularly significant,
as it has profound implications for scaling assessment approaches. This finding suggests that it is possible to
achieve strong predictive performance using a single wearable device worn by the child, rather than requiring
the more challenging and resource-intensive deployment of two separate wearables for both participants.
Our multimodal analysis further demonstrated that combining physiological and audio features may yield
marginally stronger predictive performance (F1 score of 0.92), although the added utility of multimodal signals
needs to be further researched. The ability to achieve high accuracy with physiological signals alone, however,

5.3

Ethical Considerations for In-the-Wild Deployment

The deployment of sensor-based behavioral assessment tools in home environments raises important ethical
questions that need to be carefully considered.
First, recording audio data during parent-child interactions in home environments presents substantial privacy
concerns. The intimate nature of parent-child play sessions conducted at home means that audio data inevitably
includes private family moments that the families may not have intended to share. However, our findings represent
a significant advance in the privacy-aware deployment of such assessment tools. We find that physiological
signals alone achieve comparable (F1 score of 0.86 using child wearable features) or better (F1 score of 0.91
using synchrony features) predictive performance compared to audio features (F1 score of 0.87), making them a
privacy-preserving alternative that enables families to participate in behavioral screening while maintaining
greater control over their personal information.
Second, even though Tandem achieves a high predictive performance, sensor-based behavioral assessment
cannot solely be considered an objective indicator of behavioral risk independent of context. Parent-child
interaction patterns vary significantly across cultural and socioeconomic contexts, which must be accounted for
when diagnosing or treating children for behavior problems. Our binary classification approach, while useful
for screening purposes, does not account for the complex, multidimensional nature of these interactions. In
addition, our study population was based in the United States and was predominantly white, which may limit the
generalizability of our machine learning models to other geographical and social contexts.
In light of these considerations, the responsible deployment of machine learning-based tools like Tandem
requires several safeguards. Clinical oversight must remain central to any screening process, with sensor-based
assessments serving to augment rather than replace professional judgment. We expect the deployment of such
sensor-based assessments to be done with a clinical provider in the loop to ensure that families are provided the
appropriate context and support needed after using the tool.

5.4

Opportunities for Future Work

While Tandem is a significant proof of concept for the ability to use parent-child interaction dynamics for behavior
assessment in the wild, there are opportunities for future research to both validate and improve upon the findings
presented in this work. First, while we extensively validate our feature extraction pipelines and machine learning
models, we do not optimize for the highest performing models via approaches such as hyperparameter tuning
and model selection. We instead take a conservative approach by using ensemble models and repeated k-fold
cross-validation to avoid overfitting given our relatively small sample size. Future work can explore more complex
modeling approaches to further improve classification performance.
Second, there is also an opportunity for future research to dig deeper into the DPICS interaction and physiological synchrony features that we validate here for behavior assessment. Our investigation of phase-level
models demonstrates that structured parent-child interaction sessions of shorter durations (5 or 10 minutes) could
provide enough context to derive useful dyadic features. Future work could explore the limits on the minimum
length of interaction required to obtain informative audio and/or physiological features. Similarly, researchers can
examine various ways to effectively combine dyadic features with the individual-level features used to compute
them, which would require approaches to handle multicollinearity and feature interactions. The performance of
multimodal models could also be analyzed more closely and in larger populations to determine whether audio
and physiological signals provide complementary information for behavior assessment.

CONCLUSION

This paper presents Tandem – a suite of models for automated behavior assessment in young children using
audio and/or physiological signals recorded during in-home, play-based, parent-child interactions. We conducted
a study with 68 participants (34 parent-child dyads), who completed a structured play session with child-led,
parent-led, and clean-up phases. We developed pipelines to extract meaningful markers of dyadic interaction
patterns and synchrony from smartphone-based audio and wearable-based physiological signals from both parent
and child recorded during the play session. Our analysis revealed that either audio or physiological features can
independently achieve high accuracy in detecting at-risk behavior, with audio-based DPICS interaction features
achieving an F1 score of 0.87 and physiological synchrony features achieving an F1 score of 0.91. This establishes
the feasibility of utilizing privacy-preserving physiological features for behavior assessment while maintaining
high accuracy, and provides researchers and clinicians the flexibility to choose between modalities based on
practical considerations. Our work has important implications for the child mental health and UbiComp research
communities, demonstrating the value of parent-child interaction dynamics during structured play interactions
for scalable behavior assessment.
