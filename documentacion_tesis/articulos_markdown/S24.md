Abstract

Related Work

In recent years, in addition to the aforementioned TWCs, several
interactive technologies have been proposed to support children’s
therapy and learning through play, often integrating data collection mechanisms to better understand children’s engagement and
behaviour. Some examples are discussed in this section.
Beyond autism-specific applications, data-driven games have
been explored as powerful tools for participatory sensing and usercentered design. For instance, DataPet by Dickinson et al. introduces
a mobile game principally focused at children, using virtual pets to
collect environmental data in a playful and participatory manner
[7]. Similarly, Hidayat et al. used playful probes as method for
gathering data from young users, supporting ideation and design
refinement in game development [10].
Other efforts focus not only on finding the best game design for
data acquisition, but also on behavioural monitoring and cognitive
assessment. Pandria et al. present a series (5) of pilot studies using
a series of digital platforms to track emotional states, behavioural
changes, and attention traits (e.g., ADHD), showing how gamebased systems can support diagnostic and therapeutic intervention
[16]. Likewise, Nasuha et al. explored Learning Analytics the field
AR-based games to monitor children’s engagement and inform
educational strategies through real-time performance data, showing
benefits to children and educators [15].
Finally, Wilkie et al. have proposed an inspiring approach. It
focuses on the development and validation of an observational data
collection tool designed to assess physical literacy in children during motor games, by examining the dynamic interactions between
the child and the environment. While no interactive gaming systems were employed, the use of video recording of motor games
combined with software-based event tagging, enabled teachers
to systematically collect behavioural data and monitor individual
developmental trajectories over time. [23, 24].
These few examples highlight the growing interest in combining
play, interaction, and data capture for both therapeutic and educational outcomes. However, most existing play-based data collection
systems focus on augmenting play activities or collecting singlemodal data streams outside of a clinical setting, without offering
a comprehensive, synchronized structured environment tailored
for therapeutic use with autistic children. Our work addresses this
gap by proposing a more comprehensive data collection tool, that
combines different synchronized data acquisition sources, allowing
the user to record useful data both from a toy and from a structured
environment, studying different types of behavioural interaction in
the same play-based therapy session.

1 Introduction
Autism Spectrum Condition (ASC) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by impaired or atypical behaviour in the social,
emotional, and communicative domains [1]. These impairments
typically emerge early in development and can have long-term
effects on learning, social interaction, and personal autonomy. So,
early-intervention (as early as 2 or 3 years of age) proved to be
effective in reducing the core symptoms and improving, in the long
term, the child’s social competences [6, 12, 18, 22].
Play-based activities represent a key tool in therapeutic interventions for children with ASC [13]. Through different structured
types of play, children can practice social behaviours, and express
internal emotional states. Importantly, such activities provides observable behaviours that can provide therapists with meaningful
information for assessment and treatment personalization [11, 21].
However, capturing and analysing all the relevant dimensions of
a play based therapy session, such as the child’s motor actions,
attentional focus, relationship with the therapist, and emotional
states, can be extremely challenging when relying only on therapist
observation and experience.
To address this gap, recent years have seen the emergence of
several technological devices and systems as a support to the therapists in play-based therapy sessions [3, 14]. In this context, our
research group contributed by developing the idea of Transitional
Wearable Companions (TWCs) [25]: interactive smart, soft toys
which provide sensory feedback, specifically designed to support
social engagement, while embedding sensing capabilities for behavioural data acquisition and monitoring. They include PlusMe
[20], a panda conceived to stimulate imitation, and X-8 [8], an octopus designed to stimulate turn-taking play. Lastly, we introduced
Echo, the first AI-based TWC, which is designed to recognize symbolic play activities1 [9]. While promising, these solutions mainly
focus only on child-toy interaction, without integrating other relevant behavioural data.
A step in this direction was done in [19], which introduced a
toy-based platform combining sensor data from X-8 and gaze detection on video recorded from camera glasses worn by a therapist.
This system was able to capture meaningful social interaction insights, such as joint attention2 [4]. Building on this approach, which
provided promising results, the current work aims to improve the
previous system [19], by providing a time-saving multimodal synchronized data collection tool, extendable to all TWCs, based on a
dual-camera setup and a visual synchronized platform (Figure 1).

First, we start with the description of Echo [2], the TWC used for
the play-based therapy session. Echo is a soft, smart toy prototype
developed to stimulate symbolic play activities in autistic children. It
can recognize different symbolic play activities (e.g. playing with it
by emulating an airplane or a car, or simply ignoring it) as achieved
an average accuracy of 96% on a pilot test, thanks to an embedded,
real-time Machine Learning algorithm and an IMU sensor. The toy
is designed to be easily graspable, encouraging natural interaction.
Once an activity is recognized, Echo emits light and sound feedback
to reinforce the child’s symbolic play, and logs each recognized
action with a timestamp for later analysis. Alongside with this toy,
an Android App was developed to allow the user to: Bluetooth pair
with Echo and the Camera Glasses; visualize the sensor data; save a
log file of the entire session, containing the recognized symbolic
game activity. This entire system allowed us to synchronize the
start and the stop of the toy data recording with video and sound
data coming from the two different glasses.
The Camera Glasses prototype is shown in Figure 3. It is com-

posed by a little 3D printed case that can be mounted in any commercial glasses thanks to its little shape. The hardware is based
on a XIAO ESP32S3 Sense Module board (fabricated by Seed Studio)
and a commercial OV2640-75mm camera, all powered by a 3.7 V
Li-Po Battery. This little board allowed us to manage Bluetooth
connection with the android app and mostly to save the recorded
video and audio session data on a microSD memory card, with a
resolution of 800x600 (SVGA) at 20 fps stable. Two of the these prototype were developed and they should be worn by the therapists
during the play-based therapy session. One of the therapist should
wear it near the child during the play activity to record gaze &
joint attention data, while for the other therapist we have chosen a
wide-angle camera to record the entire environment in a backward
position. This second scene in particular, could be also used in the
future to train an already developed Machine Learning-based event
detection tool based on images collected during TWC-based therapy sessions [17]. The versatility of the whole system, furthermore,
offers in the future, the possibility to connect more cameras or
more, different devices like others TWCs, in order to easily collect
– in a synchronized way – as much as possible behavioural data.

Conclusion & Future Works

In this paper, we presented a novel multimodal interactive platform
specifically designed to support play-based therapeutic sessions for
children with Autism Spectrum Conditions (ASC). By integrating
Echo, a smart toy capable of recognizing symbolic play, two Camera
Glasses, a Gaze Detection algorithm, and a Platform Application
for data visualization, our system offers a comprehensive solution
to enrich therapeutic practices with structured behavioural data.
The platform – once tested in a pilot experiment – will enables
therapists to access synchronized views of child’s behaviour, gaze
detection and interaction with the toy, potentially supporting an objective evaluation of social engagement and his/her developmental
progress.
Technical future developments will focus on: i) Camera Glasses
hardware limitations, such as extending battery life (now is about
25 minutes) and increasing video quality; ii) Platform Software
limitations, since the current system still relies on a manual workflows for saving, processing, and uploading data; our next step is to
deploy a safe, cloud-based infrastructure that will automate these
processes and ensure data is stored, organized, and accessed in a
structured, scalable, and compliant environment.
From a clinical point of view, the validation of the proposal will
require, as a next step, the design of a structured experimental protocol, developed in collaboration with neurodevelopmental therapists,
to assess its usability, effectiveness, and contribution to therapeutic
outcomes in clinical environments. In this sense, the versatility of
the proposed platform, in addition to allowing integration with
new devices and systems (e.g., other TWCs or cameras), allows it
to be shaped according to the needs of therapists.
