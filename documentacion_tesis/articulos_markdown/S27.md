ABSTRACT
Effective communication in pediatric cancer care is crucial for informed decision-making among patients, caregivers, and
healthcare providers. This scoping review assesses evidence on digital tools designed to enhance circle-of-care communication
for pediatric cancer patients and families. Eligible studies (2014–2024) evaluated digital health tools in pediatric cancer care
(with functions that targeted information exchange among patients, caregivers, or clinicians); searched via MEDLINE, Web
of Science, and EMBASE; and were in English. Twenty-eight studies were included, focusing on symptom tracking (n = 10),
quality-of-life (QOL) monitoring (n = 5), and health information sharing (n = 8). Real-time symptom-tracking tools, such as
SyMon-SAYS and RESPONSE, facilitated timely interventions by healthcare providers through automated alerts. QOL tools, like
RetinoQuest and KLIK PROM, enabled healthcare providers to integrate patient-reported outcomes into care plans. However,
patient- and caregiver-driven interventions, such as Sisom and Pain Buddy, faced challenges with adherence and accuracy
of data entry. Information-sharing platforms (MyChart, Passport for Care) centralized data, improving transitions to adult
care, but limited electronic health record integration constrained deployment. Digital tools have transformative potential for
communication in pediatric cancer care but face barriers related to adherence, data accuracy, and scalability. Future innovations
should prioritize real-time, multidisciplinary collaboration, seamless electronic health record integration, and user-centered
design.

Introduction

1.1

Rationale

Pediatric cancer care demands tightly coordinated, multidisciplinary teamwork, yet families often feel stranded in the informa-

tion gap [1]. Effective communication is crucial for coordinated
and informed decision-making and for achieving optimal participation of patients and families in healthcare [2, 3]. In this context,
the circle of care (the trusted network of healthcare professionals and support staff directly involved in a child’s diagnosis,
treatment, and follow-up) relies on seamless information flow.

1.2

Health Records

EHRs emerged in the 1960s to reduce medical errors by
standardizing documentation and improving information
sharing among providers [9]. Systems like Epic (Epic Systems
Corporation) are among the widely adopted EHR platforms
globally, offering unified access to patient data across diverse
care [9]. Patient portals, subsets of EHR systems managed by
patients and caregivers, extend these capabilities by granting
access to test results, appointments, care team information, and
discharge summaries [8, 10]. In pediatric oncology, these tools
can not only improve transparency and coordination but also
raise concerns about unfiltered results, privacy, caregiver burden,
and disparities in use [11–13]. Personal health records (PHRs), by
contrast, are independently managed by patients and families,
outside institutional EHR systems [14]. However, concerns
around data accuracy, privacy, and the digital divide remain [15].
Tethered PHRs, derived from subsets of information within the
EHR, offer a hybrid solution by supporting both patient- and
provider-supplied information [16].

1.3

Emerging Technologies

Numerous web-based tools have been developed for pediatric
cancer populations. Educational platforms aim to improve
caregiver understanding of treatment and symptom management, typically supporting one-directional communication
[17–19]. Artificial intelligence (AI), virtual environments,
and wearable devices offer new opportunities to enhance
communication across the circle of care, though integration into
clinical workflows is nascent [20, 21]. Recent reviews mapped
communication approaches for children with cancer and their
families at large [22]. One review proposed a conceptual model
for communication with children about treatment and prognosis
[23, 24]. Caregiver-facing e-communication reviews likewise
highlight usability and equity considerations [25]. Broader
pediatric telemedicine reviews report high feasibility and
satisfaction while underscoring the need for stronger outcome
evaluation [26]. Taken together, these earlier reviews establish
what families and clinicians will use; we extend this literature by

mapping which digital functions move information across teams
in pediatric oncology and where evidence remains preliminary.

1.4

Objectives

The primary objective of this scoping review is to describe
current digital health tools that aim to improve communication
between caregivers, patients, and healthcare providers.
Secondary objectives are (1) to evaluate the effectiveness of
these technologies in improving comprehension, understanding
of diagnostic and prognostic information, treatment courses,
care coordination, and informed decision-making across the
circle of care; and (2) to assess existing gaps and unmet needs in
the communication across the circle of care.

2

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI
methodology for scoping reviews [27]. Findings are reported in
accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic
Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews
(PRISMA-ScR) [28].

2.1

Eligibility Criteria

The scoping review included studies that (1) focus on pediatric
patients diagnosed with any type of cancer and/or survivors
of pediatric cancer; (2) discuss a digital health intervention,
such as websites, mobile applications, devices, software, EHRs,
telehealth platforms, online patient portals, virtual/augmented
reality; (3) specifically aimed to improve communication within
the circle of care (i.e., contained at least one feature intended
to enhance communication, such as patient/caregiver-entered
symptom/QoL data delivered to clinicians, automated alerts
to the care team, provider-to-provider coordination tools, or
portals/PHRs that enable information sharing); (4) implement
and/or evaluate outcomes of the technology described, including
feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, effectiveness, and
user satisfaction; (5) were published within the last 10 years
(2014–2024) to ensure relevance of the digital health technologies
discussed. Studies not published in English were excluded.

2.2

Information Sources

Peer-reviewed experimental, quasi-experimental, descriptive
observational, and qualitative studies indexed in biomedical
databases (MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Web of Science) were
included to ensure reproducibility of the search and provide
adequate detail for data extraction (implementation setting,
integration, and measured outcomes). We also hand-searched
reference lists from systematic reviews, relevant opinion papers,
and text articles.

and unpublished studies, with key terms relating to pediatrics,

2.4

Study Selection and Data Analysis

Citations were collated in Covidence, duplicates removed, and
titles/abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers.
Full-text screening against inclusion criteria was conducted,
with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer. Data were
extracted from papers included in the scoping review by one
reviewer using a calibrated extraction form that was tested by
the team. Extracted data included study characteristics, digital
health technology, outcomes, and limitations. A second reviewer
checked the extracted items for accuracy. Key findings were
organized into tables for clarity.

3

Results

Twenty-eight studies described digital health interventions to
improve the circle-of-care communication among pediatric cancer patients/survivors (Figure 1; Table S2). Although many
tools primarily target symptom management or psychosocial
support, they were included because they structure and transmit
patient-generated information to clinicians and/or prompt clinical review, thereby facilitating communication within the circle
of care. Detailed study characteristics are provided (Supporting
Information Table S3). A chart was generated to track the different features offered by each intervention (Supporting Information
Table S4).

3.1

Purpose of Digital Health Technologies

3.1.1

Symptom Tracking

Ten interventions enabled self-reporting and/or self-management
of symptoms [29–37]. Sisom [29, 37, 38], Color me Healthy [39],
Pain Buddy [40], and PicPecc [41] incorporate gamified features
such as drawing tools to indicate site-specific symptoms, and
custom avatars and games to guide them through symptom
reports. Sisom enhanced child-centric communication by helping
children recall and share their symptoms during clinical visits [22,
42, 43]. Gilljam et al. reported that healthcare encounters within
the Sisom intervention group were more child-centric, with
pediatricians spending more time directly engaging the children
and eliciting their views [37]. Parents found that Sisom helped
recall symptoms during clinical encounters and communicate
concerns which may otherwise be forgotten [29].

3.1.3

Sharing All Health Information

Eight digital health technologies streamline and centralize
the sharing of health information across the circle of care
[14, 49–55]. These interventions emphasize secure data sharing within the circle of care, often facilitated by cloudbased storage and role-based access. While some focus on
delivering structured, visualized treatment data, others are
more patient-centric, allowing families to manage and share
information.
SurvivorLink [49, 50], MyChart [56], and Passport for Care [57]
securely stored and shared patient data. SurvivorLink increased
awareness of specialized survivor care (75% parent and 86% survivor login rates); 90% intended continued use, and 82% found it
useful for advocating their health. MyChart facilitated transitions
to adult care, receiving high usefulness ratings (4.4/5.0), though
users were hesitant to add personal notes [56]. Passport for Care
enhanced communication for 72% of clinicians; 48% of survivors
accessing personalized care plans [57].
The Digital Treatment Summary [52] and Home Medication
Support (HoMeS) [53] provided visual summaries of treatment
information. They helped patients/families visualize treatment
protocols and manage medication schedules. By summarizing
treatment regimens into user-friendly formats, they promote
adherence, understanding, and remote provider tracking and
support. Although user-friendly, Digital Treatment Summary
had limited follow-up awareness (87.5% partially/unaware) [52],
and HoMeS, despite 100% usability ratings, did not significantly
reduce medication errors [53]. Both lacked EHR integration,
limiting impact.

Telehealth platforms emphasized real-time information
exchange through visit summaries. At the Cleveland Clinic,
Virtual Visit summaries were created and emailed to families
and clinicians; two-way information sharing was reported as a
major source of satisfaction [54]. For the Telehealth–Hospice

anxiety/uncertainty, suggesting that by addressing medical and
emotional needs, CA enhanced communication within the circle

3.1.4

Data Exchange of Digital Health Technologies

Across these interventions, data entry and sharing methods vary
with important implications for the timeliness of care, patient
autonomy, and communication across the circle of care.

3.1.5

Patient/Caregiver-Driven Data Sharing

Nine interventions relied on the patient or caregiver to initiate
data entry and sharing across the circle of care. This supports
autonomy and in-visit discussion but risks underreporting and
delays when information is conveyed only at appointments. Of
these, five applications focus on data entry primarily by children and sharing information by themselves or their caregivers,
including Sisom [29, 37, 38], Color Me Healthy [59], PicPecc [41],
CAC [46], and Rizvi et al. [43]. These interventions focus on
pediatric patients and rely on child-driven data entry with support
from caregivers to share information during encounters. Patients
or caregivers must share reports during appointments, placing
responsibility on families; this empowers control over healthcare
interactions but risks underreporting or delays due to reliance on
clinical encounters rather than real-time updates.
C-SCAT [36] and Kræftværket [44] target adolescents and older
pediatric cancer patients, who are often more independent in
managing care. Adolescents handle data entry and sharing
themselves. The onus is on the patient to bring this information
forward during clinical encounters. This model emphasizes
independence and supports transition to adult care but may cause
variability in how well data are shared with the healthcare team.
Child SMILE [34] and CA [58] rely solely on caregivers to
enter/share health data. The caregiver-driven model eases communication with providers but may cause bottlenecks or variability based on caregiver availability, digital literacy, or engagement.

3.1.7

Real-Time Data Sharing

Eight interventions enabled real-time patient data sharing across
the circle of care, allowing provider access to patient/caregiverentered information. This model reduces the reliance on scheduled encounters for sharing health information. Automated
data sharing helps care teams access patient-reported outcomes
without manual uploads or reminders.
Remote Symptom Management in Pediatric Oncology
(RESPONSE) [35], SyMon-SAYS [31, 32], CanSelfMan [30],
and Pain Buddy [40] feature real-time automated alerts to
healthcare providers when thresholds (e.g., symptom severity)
are met. Patients/caregivers enter data, which are automatically
processed and shared with providers through a web-based
dashboard. RetinoQuest [45], KLIK PROM [39], and MyPalChild [47, 48] emphasize real-time sharing, automatically
process data, and present it to healthcare providers through an
automatic web-based dashboard.
SurvivorLink [49, 50] and MyChart [56] are electronic PHR
systems that allow survivors, caregivers, and healthcare providers
to share and access health information in real time. SurvivorLink
relies on survivor entry, while MyChart data are healthcare
provider-entered. Both improve patient engagement and ensure
continuity of care across settings.

3.2

Gaps in Current Digital Health Technologies

3.2.1

Reliance on Patient-Driven Communication

Many interventions relied on consistent and accurate patient
or caregiver data entry. Sisom and Pain Buddy suffered low
adherence from patient fatigue or loss of interest; despite Sisom’s
high usability (97%), 15% of children did not complete tasks [40,
60]. Pain Buddy’s low adherence limited timely interventions
[40]. PicPecc gave children autonomy over symptom tracking,
but some children felt their communication was not reciprocated
by nurses when symptoms were severe [41]. HoMeS faced
similar caregiver adherence challenges, with only 1/3 of parents
consistently bringing medication calendars to visits [53]. Such a
disconnect risks missing critical health data or delayed actions
when responsibility falls on the patient.

3.2.2

Lack of Integration

Real-time tools like SyMon-SAYS and C-SCAT were often not
integrated into EHRs used by clinicians, resulting in missed
opportunities for timely intervention and cohesion in the circle
of care [32, 36]. Applications on external, third-party software
potentially serve as barriers for clinician access, reducing provider
6 of 9

engagement. For example, despite generating hundreds of alerts,
SyMon-SAYS had only 52% clinician documentation of actions
[32]. C-SCAT faced similar issues, where healthcare providers
documented only 33.7% management plans for priority symptoms
[36]. Similarly, RetinoQuest and KLIK PROM allowed realtime data processing and sharing with healthcare providers, but
limited integration into EHRs reduced the potential for seamless
communication [45].

3.2.3

Limited Circle-of-Care Coverage

Circle-of-care coverage, defined as the number of institutions in
which the interventions were implemented, varied significantly
and directly impacted communication. Sisom engaged children,
parents, and clinicians, limited to 2–4 pediatric hospitals. Color
Me Healthy and Pain Buddy were effective in single pediatric
oncology clinics, but did not engage a larger circle of care [40,
59]. RESPONSE and Child SMILE, each in a single pediatric
hospital, showed limited scalability [34, 35]. Although C-SCAT
and SyMon-SAYS demonstrated broader geographic reach in five
oncology clinics and one hospital’s Epic EHR, the lack of broader
integration hampered assessment of real-time communication
[32, 36]. In contrast, Passport for Care covered 146 clinics and
showed comprehensive coverage of the healthcare team [57].
Overall, fragmentation and lack of broad integration across
hospitals limited the engagement of the entire circle of care.

4

Discussion

This scoping review profiles 28 innovative digital tools designed
to connect pediatric cancer patients, caregivers, and providers.
Symptom-tracking apps with real-time alerts (SyMon-SAYS,
RESPONSE, and CanSelfMan) cut caregiver burden and accelerate clinical response to acute changes [30, 32, 35]. Gamified
platforms (Sisom and Pain Buddy) drove engagement but faltered
on sustained data entry [42, 43]. QOL systems with formal
assessments (RetinoQuest and KLIK PROM) delivered actionable
patient-reported data in up to 80% of consultations, boosting
care planning [39, 45]. PHRs and patient portals (SurvivorLink,
MyChart, and Passport for Care) secured broad access to critical
information, yet privacy and EHR integration gaps persist [14, 50,
57]. While recent syntheses catalogued communication supports
for children with cancer and families [22–24], those overviews
established what tools exist and how users perceive them; our
contribution is narrower and more technical: we focus on digital
interventions and analyze information routing and deployment
scale, extending portal/survivorship literatures beyond access
and satisfaction to who enters data, who sees it, and whether it
triggers action [10].
Our primary objective, to map tools improving circle-of-care
communication, reveals a fragmented landscape: direct realtime data sharing excels when seamlessly integrated; patientdriven platforms engage but lack reliability; comprehensive
information-sharing achieves scale but faces privacy and usability
hurdles. To bridge these gaps, future solutions must merge realtime interoperability with user-centered design, ensuring every
voice, child, caregiver, and clinician is heard in concert. This

and burden minimization, brief, just-in-time prompts; thresholdbased check-ins rather than fixed schedules; role-based forms
for children vs. caregivers; (iii) closed-loop feedback, automated
acknowledgments or short clinician notes after alerts to signal
reciprocity; and (iv) onboarding and micro-training for families
and clinicians, including quickstart videos, tip sheets, and inclinic set-up (leveraging caregiver-facing contexts such as the
WeChat Mini Program and CA [34, 61]). Collectively, these
approaches align inputs with clinical action, which our synthesis
suggests is critical for continued engagement.
Developing a unified communication hub that merges patient
portals, PHRs, web applications, and telehealth into one cloudbased interface will break down silos, giving children, families,
and providers a shared, real-time view of health information. Future work should prioritize comparative designs, longer
follow-up, and predefined process and clinical endpoints. To
avoid widening the digital divide, they should also provide
multilingual interfaces and plain-language/low-literacy content;
include audio/icon-supported reporting for younger users; support offline/low-bandwidth modes or SMS fallbacks; consider
loaner devices/data vouchers where feasible; and monitor equitysensitive uptake (i.e., by language, bandwidth, and device access)
as a core implementation outcome. Finally, scaling deployments
from single sites to regional networks and implementing realtime dashboards to monitor adherence, alert response rates, and
user satisfaction will drive continuous improvement.

Conclusion

Digital health tools, from real-time trackers to EHR-integrated
platforms, are well-positioned to improve circle-of-care communication by surfacing patient-reported data in visits, prompting
timely review, and supporting care transitions in pediatric
oncology. Yet the current evidence base is early-stage and heterogeneous and limited adherence, data accuracy gaps, and siloed
workflows limit their reach. Embedding these solutions into
existing systems, championing user-centered, scalable designs,
and securing clinician buy-in will create a connected, efficient
circle of care from diagnosis through survivorship.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. Included studies had
high heterogeneity in the types of interventions employed and
outcome measures collected, which inhibited direct comparisons
and syntheses. This review included only interventions targeting
pediatric cancer populations, limiting the generalizability of findings to other patient groups or broader healthcare contexts. Many
included articles lacked implementation data, such as usability
assessments, user engagement metrics, or cost-effectiveness. Our
search was restricted to articles available in English, which may
bias the evidence base toward English-language scholarship. We
focused on biomedical databases, and some relevant studies
may therefore be absent. Studies published earlier than 2014
were not included, as platforms and deployment contexts have
changed substantially. Collectively, these choices favor recency
and clinical implementation over exhaustive coverage. Finally,
the body of literature may be affected by publication bias, as
