ABSTRACT
Objective: This study focused on in-home use of a tele-assessment tool for autism diagnosis in young children, the TELE-ASD-

PEDS (TAP). Psychometric properties, caregiver experiences, and perceptions of feasibility were examined among families likely to
experience barriers to in-person evaluation.
Methods: One hundred eighty-two children between 18 and 42 months of age were recruited because of positive screening,
primary care concerns, or early intervention referral. All participants completed initial tele-assessment including the TAP. Approximately 2 weeks later, a second visit was conducted: 92 were randomized to a repeat TAP administration by telehealth and 90 were
randomized to an in-person evaluation. Caregivers completed surveys regarding challenges with technology and satisfaction with
telehealth assessment.
Results: Overall, 77% of the sample was diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder (n 5 140). There were few diagnostic disagreements (n 5 10, 6%) between initial and second evaluations, with disagreements equally distributed between second visit type.
Diagnostic outcomes (autism vs no autism) agreed between telehealth and in-person evaluation for 94% of cases, kappa 5 0.82.
Outcomes agreed between 2 telehealth visits for 94% of cases, kappa 5 0.84. Test-retest reliability of total TAP scores across 2
administrations was strong, intraclass correlation coefficient 5 0.85. Very few caregivers reported challenges with technology during
telehealth appointments (,6%); 92% reported that there was nothing they would change about the telehealth visit. Examiners also
reported high satisfaction with telehealth assessments.
Conclusion: In-home use of the TAP is valid, reliable, feasible, and acceptable to caregivers. Findings support the continued use of

telehealth assessment for autism in toddlers, which can reduce disparities in access to timely diagnostic services.
Index terms: autism spectrum disorder, telehealth, evaluation, TELE-ASD-PEDS, reliability

Telemedicine is increasingly used to evaluate young children
for possible autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Telemedicinebased assessment (hereafter “tele-assessment”) has been
offered for many years to reduce wait times, travel burden,
and other barriers families experience when seeking autism
evaluation.1,2 Tele-assessment was increasingly used during the
Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, particularly
when in-person care was suspended.3 As pandemic precautions
have receded, tele-assessment is used less frequently but has
remained as an alternative to in-person autism evaluations for
families who experience logistical, financial, or resourcerelated barriers to accessing care.4 Despite frequent use, limited
data exist on the psychometric properties of tele-assessment

tools and family experiences of tele-assessments provided in
their homes.
Broadly, tele-assessment refers to the provision of assessment
services remotely, with a patient in 1 location communicating
with a provider virtually. Initial tele-assessment approaches to
autism assessment included coaching caregivers to administer
existing assessment tools5 or having paraprofessionals in
community-based settings (e.g., regional health clinics, schools)
administer tests while a psychologist observed remotely.6
Although both approaches yielded high rates of diagnostic
agreement with in-person assessment,6 limitations related to need
for prior training and specific assessment materials affected the
scalability and ultimate utility of these methods.

The TELE-ASD-PEDS (TAP)7 was created specifically for the
purpose of remote assessment of young children with concern
for possible autism.8 The TAP is a caregiver-led, clinicianguided assessment that allows for observation of a child’s
social, communication, and play skills during interactive
activities with a primary caregiver. It was designed to be brief,
use materials available in most families’ homes, and include
activities that caregivers could administer with minimal
advance preparation. The TAP was initially investigated in
controlled research settings,9 in which families completed the
TAP in a clinic room with provided materials while the
examiner observed by telehealth on a computer from a different
room. In such settings, it had high levels of diagnostic
agreement with in-person assessments, clinician diagnostic
certainty, and family and clinician satisfaction. The TAP was
then disseminated broadly at the onset of the COVID-19
pandemic and quickly employed for direct-to-home use, but
few studies have examined its psychometric properties or
family satisfaction when used in homes and administered by
caregivers with clinician guidance.10,11
A range of tele-assessment tools now exist for use with
individuals across the age range.12,13 However, most of these
assessment tools have limited psychometric data at present. There
remains a need both for validating tele-assessment tools
specifically for use within direct-to-home settings and for
understanding any unique challenges and benefits to assessing
children within their homes. The present study seeks to address
these gaps by presenting data from a study of tele-assessment
using the TAP in direct-to-home settings, including (1) psychometric properties of in-home use in a broader community sample
with novel groups of diagnostic clinicians and (2) caregiver
experiences and perceptions of the feasibility of in-home use
among rural and underresourced families who may experience
barriers to in-person evaluation.

METHODS
This study presents interim analysis from an ongoing 2-site
(Vanderbilt University Medical Center [VUMC] in Tennessee and
UC Davis MIND Institute in California [UCD]) clinical trial of
ASD diagnosis using a direct-to-home telemedicine tool, the TAP
(clinicaltrials.gov, NCT05047224). The larger ongoing trial will
also examine families’ ability to engage with services after
telehealth evaluations by following them for a 6-month period
after evaluation. We specifically targeted for recruitment families
from geographically diverse and underserved areas. All participants completed an initial tele-assessment by Zoom. They were
then randomized to a second visit that occurred either by
telehealth platform again (n 5 92; to assess test-retest reliability
and ultimately understand the impact of solely receiving telemedicine diagnostic services in real-world settings) or in-person
(n 5 90; to assess convergent validity between telehealth and inperson evaluations). Examiners for all evaluations were Master’s
or PhD level with extensive experience in ASD in-person and teleevaluation of young children; all examiners were either licensed
or supervised by a licensed psychologist. The study was approved
by the universities’ Institutional Review Boards and parents/
guardians provided informed consent.

Measures
Tele-assessment Measures
TELE-ASD-PEDS The TAP is a tool developed specifically for
remote assessment of autism-related behaviors in young children.7 It consists of 8 activities: free play and joint play, physical
play routines (e.g., peekaboo, tickling), social responsivity
(i.e., name calling and directing attention), activities that may
prompt a child to request (e.g., snack), anticipation-building activity (e.g., blowing bubbles, rolling a ball back and forth), and
planned ignoring. All TAP administrations were conducted
within the family’s home, using whatever toys and materials were
available in the family’s home. Clinicians guided caregivers
through the activities and observed while the caregiver engaged
the child. After administration, which takes 20 to 30 minutes,
clinicians rated child behavior on 7 items (i.e., socially directed
speech and sounds, use of eye contact, atypical vocalizations,
unusual or repetitive play, unusual or repetitive body movements,
use of gestures and integration with eye contact/vocalization, and
unusual sensory exploration or reaction), using a 3-point scale
(3 5 behaviors characteristic of ASD clearly present; 2 5 possible
atypical behavior; 1 5 behaviors characteristic of ASD not present). A total score of 12 or higher indicates likelihood of ASD.15
All study examiners were trained in TAP administration by the
measure’s developers; initial cross-site reliability was established,
and regular cross-site calibration was conducted.
Developmental Profile, Fourth Edition The Developmental
Profile, Fourth Edition (DP-4) is a caregiver report of developmental skills in 5 areas: Physical, Adaptive Behavior,
Social-Emotional, Cognitive, and Communication.16 The DP-4
uses a dichotomous yes/no response format, with each question
asking whether a child has mastered a particular skill. Normsbased standard scores are provided for each scale from birth
through 21 years, 11 months. The interview version of the DP-4
was used.

Clinical Interview Clinicians completed a Diagnostic and
Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-based interview
that gathered information about autism-related characteristics
and behaviors. Clinicians were provided a guide containing
questions related to DSM criteria, although interviews were
allowed to vary among clinicians as clinically appropriate.
In-person Assessment Measures
Mullen Scales of Early Learning The Mullen Scales of Early
Learning (MSEL) is a standardized, normed developmental
assessment for children 1 to 68 months.17 Four subscales were
administered at the in-person visit, each yielding a T score using
published norms: Visual Reception, Fine Motor, Receptive Language, and Expressive Language. The MSEL also provides a
standard score representing an overall index of ability based on
the 4 administered subscales, the Early Learning Composite.
Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Third Edition The Vineland
Adaptive Behavior Scales, Third Edition (VABS-3) is a caregiverreport instrument that assesses adaptive functioning in social,
communication, motor, and daily living skills.18 Norms-based
standard scores are provided for birth through adulthood for
each domain, and an overall Adaptive Behavior Composite score.
Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition The
Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition
(ADOS-2) is a semi-structured play-based interaction and observation designed to assess symptoms of ASD, with distinct
modules designed for use based on a child’s language level and
age.19 Calibrated severity scores, ranging from 1 to 10, allow for
comparison of ADOS-2 scores across different administration
modules, with scores of 4 and above indicative of autism.20 All
examiners had achieved research reliability on the ADOS-2;

Questionnaires
Clinician Perception Questionnaire After each visit (both teleand in-person assessments), clinicians completed a questionnaire
documenting their diagnostic impression (autism vs no autism),
diagnostic certainty, and a clinical best estimate impression inclusive of differential diagnoses. Clinicians were also asked to
record any factors affecting the assessment (e.g., child behaviors,
caregiver behaviors, technology issues) and their overall satisfaction with the assessment. The clinician questionnaire was
based on past published work.15
Caregiver Satisfaction Questionnaire After each visit, caregivers
completed a questionnaire assessing perceptions of assessment
procedures, including ease of completing activities, clarity of
instructions, length of visit, satisfaction with the child behaviors
elicited by activities, and overall comfort with the process.9,15
Questions also asked whether the assessment process met the
family’s needs, whether their questions were answered, whether
they found the recommendations provided useful, and whether
the evaluation supported their understanding of their child’s
diagnosis and resources available. The questionnaire also included questions allowing for open-ended feedback.
Procedure
Initial Tele-assessment. All families completed an initial
diagnostic tele-assessment appointment that included the TAP,
DP-4, and DSM-based clinical interview. Tele-assessment was
completed by Zoom and generally lasted between 1.5 and 2.5
hours.

Diagnostic Determination. After telehealth assessments were
completed, the virtual appointment was paused while examiners
scored assessments and formulated their diagnostic impression.
Examiners determined whether the child met criteria for ASD or
not, and if not, what the clinical best estimate diagnosis/outcome
was. If the examiner was unable to reach a determination about
ASD based on the initial telehealth visit, this was documented,
and the diagnostic decision was deferred to the second visit. Examiners then delivered feedback to families, answered questions,
and provided recommendations and resources based on the
child’s diagnosis/outcome. After the assessment, examiners prepared reports documenting diagnosis and recommendations,
except in the case of deferred diagnoses when the report could be
provided after the second visit only. Reports were written by or
under the supervision of licensed psychologists and shared with
families both by mail and e-mail, following Institutional Review
Board-approved procedures.

Data Availability. Data included in this study have been
submitted to the National Database for Autism Research.

Follow-up Assessment. At the conclusion of the initial teleassessment visit (after diagnostic decision-making was completed), families were randomized to complete either (1) an inperson evaluation (TAP-IP group) or (2) a briefer second teleassessment appointment (TAP-TELE group). Second appointments were completed 2 to 3 weeks after initial assessment
(mean 5 20.08 days; range 5 1–83 days; SD 5 12.58). Eight
participants were lost to follow-up after the first visit and not seen
for the second visit (6 had been randomized to TAP-IP, 2 randomized to TAP-TELE). Second appointments were completed by
an examiner unaware of the first evaluation’s outcome. Participants in the TAP-IP group were administered the MSEL and
ADOS-2, and their caregivers completed the Vineland. Clinical
(DSM-based) questions were asked to confirm diagnostic impressions as needed. TAP-IP appointments were completed at the
main study sites (mirroring the travel expectations families often
face when accessing in-person evaluations outside of the study).
The TAP-TELE group completed a second telemedicine appointment focused on repeat administration of the TAP.
Diagnostic Confirmation. After testing for the in-person or telehealth follow-up was completed, the appointment was paused
while examiners scored assessments and formulated their diagnostic impression. Only after making a diagnostic classification
of ASD or not ASD did examiners then review the diagnostic
outcome from the first telehealth visit. If the diagnostic classifications agreed, this was shared with the family and considered the
child’s final study outcome. If the second examiner’s impression
differed from the first, the clinician was able to review the report
and any notes from the first examiner, gather additional information if needed, and consult with the first examiner. The
impressions were shared with families, a consensus diagnostic
determination was reached between the examiners and the family, and the consensus determination was considered the child’s
final study outcome.
Longitudinal Follow-up Surveys. After the completion of both
study visits, participants complete questionnaires (delivered by
mobile device) over the following 6 months regarding service
access. At the end of the 6-month follow-up period, families
complete a final survey regarding their experience with telehealth
assessment. Collection of these data is ongoing and not included
in the current analyses.

RESULTS
Demographics
Demographic information is presented in Table 1. We recruited a
sample that was diverse not only in race and ethnicity but also in
socioeconomic status and geography. The sample was 26%
Hispanic/Latino and 32% non-White, with 60% of primary
caregivers without a college degree. Families came from a range of
income levels, with 65% below their state’s median family income
(calculated based on 2023 Census Bureau median family income
for each state for a family of 4). Many lived in rural parts of their
states, averaging over 50 miles away from (and over a 1-hourlong drive to) the medical center. There were no significant
differences between children with and without ASD outcomes in
demographic characteristics (i.e., child sex, ethnicity, race,
primary caregiver education, annual household income, and
distance from medical center) or visit characteristics (i.e., age at
first visit, days between study visits). As would be expected,
children with ASD outcomes had lower scores than children
without ASD outcomes on all measures of development and
adaptive behavior (i.e., DP-4, MSEL, VABS-3 subscale and
composite scores). Demographic information by study site can be
found in Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/
JDBP/A490.
Diagnostic Agreement Across Visits
One hundred eighty-two children completed both evaluations: 92
randomized to a telehealth second visit and 90 randomized to an
in-person second visit. Overall, 77% were diagnosed with ASD
(n 5 140). Non-ASD outcomes (the examiner’s clinical best
estimate based on all information obtained during the evaluation)
included global developmental delay, speech/language delay,
subthreshold social communication and/or repetitive behavior
concerns, attention/activity level concerns, other externalizing
problems (disruptive/oppositional behavior), and no concerns/
typically developing. As seen in Table 2, there were few diagnostic
disagreements (n 5 10, 6%) between the initial and the second
evaluations, and disagreements were equally distributed between
second visit type (i.e., n 5 5 telehealth, n 5 5 in-person). The
diagnostic outcome from the initial telehealth visit agreed with the
final outcome determination for 97% of cases.

DISCUSSION
Although the TAP has previously shown strong agreement with
traditional in-person assessment when used in controlled clinic
settings,15 this study is the first rigorous evaluation of in-home use
of the TAP. We examined convergent validity, test-retest reliability,
feasibility, and caregiver acceptability of telehealth evaluation
using the TAP across 2 sites among a racially, ethnically,
geographically, and economically diverse sample. The current
study included children recruited through several referral pathways
to best reflect how children in the broader community come to seek
autism evaluation (through screening, referral by primary care
providers/clinicians, and early intervention referral). The TAP
showed excellent convergent validity when compared with
traditional in-person instruments (i.e., ADOS-2) and high testretest reliability across repeat administrations with different
examiners. The strong psychometric properties of in-home use of
the TAP for evaluation of ASD in toddlers indicate that it is a
promising method to increase access to diagnostic services,
particularly for rural and/or underserved communities.
Diagnostic agreement between the initial telehealth evaluation
and either a repeat TAP administration or an in-person evaluation
using the ADOS-2 was 94%. This is comparable with studies of inperson diagnostic classification using repeat ADOS administrations and video review of ADOS administrations.19,21 Intraclass
correlation coefficients between scores from repeat TAP administrations were in the same range or higher than those between repeat
administrations of the ADOS-2, using module scores22 or
calibrated severity scores,23 and in the range indicating good
reliability.24 This suggests that diagnostic accuracy of telehealth
evaluation using the TAP is satisfactory and comparable with the
diagnostic accuracy of traditional in-person assessment using goldstandard measures of observed behavior (i.e., ADOS-2).
Diagnostic disagreements between initial and second visits
were rare and equally distributed between second visit type. This
suggests that disagreements were not driven by the method of the
second assessment (telehealth or in-person) but may instead
reflect characteristics of the child (e.g., emerging symptoms,
borderline presentation). This is also the case for traditional inperson evaluations, in which agreement between evaluators on
diagnostic classification is lower for children presenting with
complicated presentations, higher adaptive functioning, and
milder symptoms.25 In the future work from this study, we will
investigate in detail the characteristics of children with diagnostic
disagreements to better understand for whom telehealth evaluation may be most and least optimal.
Cases in which clinicians felt unable to make a diagnostic
decision (i.e., ASD or no ASD) from the initial telehealth visit were
rare—most cases (92%) had a diagnostic determination from
telehealth alone. This indicates that for most young children
(18–42 months) in need of an autism evaluation, telehealth
assessment using the TAP will provide sufficient clinical information to reach a diagnostic decision. Providing a telehealth

Our findings also highlight the feasibility and value of using
telehealth evaluations in families’ homes. By design, the TAP is
flexible in administration and can be conducted with toys and
items already available in the family’s home. All families in our
sample, across the socioeconomic spectrum, had sufficient
availability of materials to complete the TAP. Other telehealth
instruments require clinicians to buy and mail kits of specific
materials to families26 or for families to videotape themselves or
have a staff member come to the home to record observations of
daily activities.27 By contrast, the TAP places a low burden on
both families and clinicians by using materials already in the
home and needing minimal previsit preparation, as caregivers are
talked through the TAP activities live.
Families were intentionally recruited from a wide range of
geographic areas, including rural regions where internet stability
could potentially be less reliable, to evaluate whether issues arose
with the use of technology. All families had adequate internet
connectivity or cellular service to complete visits, very few
participants reported difficulties related to technology during
telehealth visits, and no technology difficulties prevented the
completion of a visit. Similar findings of feasibility have been
reported in a small pilot study using in-home TAP assessment in
place of in-person assessment within a diagnostic practice,28 in
which all participating families were able to complete telehealth
assessment, caregivers reported positive experiences, and clinicians were able to gather information needed to make a diagnostic
determination and in some cases even observe behavior that
would have been difficult to see in an office setting. In addition to
the feasibility of telehealth, our study also highlights the potential
travel burden families face to access diagnostic services that
would be eased by telehealth. On average, families lived over an
hour’s drive—1 way—to a diagnostic center, and as far as almost
4 hours 1 way. Such travel times would require families to take a
full day off work and/or school and incur large travel costs (e.g.,
gas, hotel stay) to access in-person diagnostic services, burdens
alleviated by telehealth services.
In addition to high feasibility ratings for telehealth evaluations,
caregivers reported satisfaction and positive feelings about
evaluations by telehealth. After completing the initial telehealth
evaluation, most caregivers reported that the examiner was able
to observe their child’s concerning behaviors and that they felt
comfortable receiving feedback about their child over telehealth.
Most caregivers also reported that they would recommend
participating in this kind of evaluation to others. This extends
previous findings of positive feedback from caregivers about such
tele-assessment tools administered in a clinic setting9 and high
rates of caregiver satisfaction with telehealth evaluation using the
TAP implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic.29,30 Caregiver satisfaction with telehealth evaluation is crucial to its
continued use and critical to ensuring a comparable quality of
care to in-person assessment. Caregiver feedback demonstrates
that there is a very large portion of families in need of ASD
evaluation for their toddlers for whom telehealth is a satisfactory,
and sometimes even preferable, option.
Although we were able to enroll families living in 2 different
states, we were unable to enroll families living outside of those
states, which may limit the generalizability of findings. Another
limitation of the current results is that they report only immediate

impressions of telehealth. It is also important to understand longterm perceptions of families and their ability to access services.
We are collecting longitudinal follow-up data from families for
6 months after study evaluations, querying service access and
long-term impressions of their telehealth experiences. Future
work will probe caregiver responses and reactions immediately
after telehealth evaluation in greater detail, and investigate longterm impressions.
Overall, results from this study indicate that in-home use of the
TAP during telehealth evaluation of ASD in toddlers is valid,
reliable, feasible, and acceptable to caregivers—including in rural
and underserved communities with limited access to in-person
services. Recent work has also shown that caregiver report of
developmental functioning is a suitable proxy for direct developmental testing in the context of telehealth evaluations for
ASD in young children (Ozonoff et al.31). Taken together, these
findings support the continued use of telehealth assessment for
ASD in toddlers, providing an opportunity to expand timely and
convenient access to diagnostic services for families and reduce
disparities in access.
