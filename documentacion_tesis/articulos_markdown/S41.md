Microservices emerged as a popular architectural style over the last decade. Although designed to be selfcontained, microservices must communicate to realize business capabilities, creating dependencies among
their data and functionalities. Developers then resort to asynchronous, event-based communication to fulfill
such dependencies while reducing coupling. However, developers are often oblivious to the inherent challenges
of the event-based communication paradigm, leading to pitfalls that ultimately prompt them to reconsider
adopting microservices. To make matters worse, the literature is scarce on the practices and challenges of
designing, implementing, testing, monitoring, and troubleshooting event-based microservices.
To fill this gap, this article provides the first comprehensive characterization of event management practices
and challenges in microservices, based on a repository-mining study of over 8,000 Stack Overflow questions. We
find that developers encounter a gamut of problems, including handling large event payloads, modeling event
schemas, auditing and debugging event flows, and managing event dependencies and ordering constraints,
suggesting that current practices and technologies do not sufficiently serve developers. We review the state of
the art and practice through the lens of unveiled challenges and provide actionable implications for developers,
providers, and researchers. As a result, our study will support advancing event management solutions in
microservices.

Introduction

The emergence of cloud computing as a paradigm for large-scale deployment of services has
prompted industry practitioners to rethink how applications are architected and deployed [6].
In particular, we witness a growing adoption of microservice architectures [222]. Microservice
architectures promote designing components as independent building blocks that are deployed
independently and interact with each other via network protocols [74]. This architectural style brings
more flexibility for the development and deployment of large systems, in contrast to conventional
architectures, in which software components are strongly coupled with each other and deployed as
a whole unit [140].
The main benefit of microservices is that development teams can develop and operate their
services independently [210]. Microservices should be designed as self-contained software units that
operate autonomously [140]. To realize business capabilities, microservices need to communicate,
which ends up creating dependencies among their data and functionalities [115]. To manage
such dependencies while maximizing decoupling, developers often rely on asynchronous eventbased communication between microservices, instead of using traditional remote procedure calls
[110, 114, 147]. The rationale is that, through events, producers and consumers are not directly
coupled. For instance, producer microservices can trigger operations in other microservices or
communicate their own state updates through events. Such an approach may positively influence
the application’s ability to evolve over time, limit the propagation of faults, and favor increased
scalability of individual components [110, 154].
Event-Driven Architecture (EDA) has been rapidly gaining industry popularity [25]. However, despite the benefits of an event-driven design, developers frequently report challenges in
managing events in industrial settings [32, 59, 81, 114, 115, 142, 143, 147, 175, 176, 179, 180, 217].
Not surprisingly, it is common to encounter developers seeking support on how to implement
event-driven microservices appropriately, such as this comment found on Stack Overflow (SO) (a
popular Questions and Answers forum) [40]: “Implementing an eventually consistent distributed
architecture has turned out to be a pain. There are tons of blog posts telling stories about how to do
it, but not showing (code) how to actually do it. One of the aspects I’m suffering is having to deal
with manual retries of the messages when they haven’t been acknowledged.” To make matters worse,
in our study, we observed that there is a scarcity of literature on the practices and challenges
that the adoption of an asynchronous paradigm brings to the designing, implementing, testing,
monitoring, and troubleshooting of microservices. The challenges of managing communication
in asynchronous and event-based systems have been explored in the fields of databases and distributed systems [28, 202], but this knowledge is not widely disseminated among developers [23,
115], making them sometimes try to “reinvent the wheel” when addressing common challenges.
This limited understanding of existing practices and how to address challenges in architectures
based on asynchronous communication leads to frustrations and ultimately makes companies
reconsider the adoption of microservices [83, 129, 188].
There has been extensive work investigating the adoption and implications of microservice
architectures [15, 57, 115, 130, 138, 162, 201, 208, 209, 219, 221]. However, these pieces of work
mostly focus on Representational State Transfer (REST) architectural style, describe the benefits

This work aims to characterize the practices and challenges faced by developers when adopting

technological trends and common implementation patterns related to the adoption of event management. We also seek to identify the functional requirement and Non-Functional Requirement

existing practices for event management in microservices and make them aware of the challenges
they will face when developing asynchronous and event-based microservices.
To achieve our goal, we perform, to the best of our knowledge, the first empirical study to comprehend the practices and identify the challenges in managing events in microservice architectures
based on discussions of developers. To this end, our study is based on repository mining [82].
We mine and analyze relevant questions from SO [185], one of the most popular Questions and
Answers forums for developers who seek technical advice or assistance [31]. We collect more than
8,000 SO questions associated with managing events in microservice architectures. Via a mix of
keyword analysis and manual examination of tags, questions, and related responses (i.e., posts), we
collect the most popular patterns and NFR and functional requirement mentioned in the context
of event management in microservices. Then, we randomly sample 628 relevant SO questions for
manual analysis to identify the challenges. We carefully extract the challenges behind each question
through a peer-reviewed process. We classify the uncovered challenges into different properties of
distributed systems and functional requirement and NFR. These ultimately represent key areas that
microservice developers struggle with.
In the quantitative analysis, we observe that event management has been gaining increasing
attention in practice, indicating the emergence of this architectural paradigm and the timeliness
of this study. Results show the popularity of patterns and requirements related to achieving data
consistency and loose coupling. This suggests microservice developers seek to strike a better
balance between consistency and decoupling in their microservice architectures by employing
asynchronous events, especially in computations that span microservices. Furthermore, we observe
that developers report different categories of patterns in their implementations. For instance,
observability (e.g., distributed tracing), performance (e.g., circuit breaker), and security (e.g., access
token) patterns are reported to be applied in questions involving event management.
In the qualitative analysis, we find that microservice practitioners face a myriad of challenges in
managing events. While events are supposed to maximize the decoupling of microservices [69, 114],
developers often surprisingly necessitate synchronizing events for correct event processing and
coordinating microservices to allow for software evolution, contradicting the alleged benefits of an
event-based architecture [72, 154]. Besides, although events can serve as natural progress markers of
microservices [72, 110], we observe that developers find little benefit in using events to monitor and
troubleshoot microservices. In particular, developers have a hard time tracking down the result of

Background and Motivation

2.1

Microservice Architectures

The microservice architectural style arose as a popular strategy for building applications in the cloud
[222]. In contrast to the conventional monolithic architecture, where application components are
strongly coupled and communicate with each other via function calls under a single execution
unit, a microservice architecture promotes high modularity, designing applications as independent
components that interact with each other via mechanisms such as HTTP-based protocols or
asynchronous messages [140].
The decoupled design promoted by microservice architectures exhibits both advantages and disadvantages. On the one hand, microservices can be polyglot, using different programming languages
and independent databases [115]. Besides, microservices promote fault isolation, where faults can
be constrained to an individual microservice boundary, e.g., through a container-based deployment.
The latter refrains failures from propagating to other components of the architecture [71].
2.2

Despite being designed to operate autonomously, microservices often show data and functionality
dependencies [115]. To manage these dependencies while maximizing decoupling, practitioners

“Rethinking service interactions as streams of event exchanges (as opposed to a sequence of

An event serves as a logical unit of communication across microservices. It often carries a payload
containing useful semantic data about the behavior of the producer microservice (e.g., invoice
issued) or the expected outcome of the consumer microservices (e.g., process payment) [69]. Besides
communicating actions, events are leveraged by large-scale architectures to aid state management,
as noted below:
“Events are much more than triggers and updates. They become the immutable stream from
which we can reconstruct the entire system state. Moving towards a publish/subscribe model
enables every service to publish its changes as events into a message bus, which can then be
consumed by another service of interest that needs to adjust its state of the world” [172].
To illustrate the role events play in microservice architectures, we use Figure 1 to illustrate
an e-commerce platform. We get inspiration from a popular microservice open source project
[5] that incorporates real-world event management patterns. The application is composed of the
following microservices: Cart, entailed for keeping track of user’s cart items, Stock, responsible
for managing the availability of products; Order responsible for the processing of orders, Payment
processes the payment of order invoices, and Product, that maintains products’ information. The
microservices rely on a message broker to communicate with each other through asynchronous
events (represented with a letter figure). The events cut across several microservices and serve as
the building blocks for composing the functionalities of different microservices.
Upon a customer’s checkout request, the Cart microservice spawns the processing of a checkout
by generating a checkout_cart event. The event is then consumed by the Order microservice, which
processes the cart items and generates the place_order event. Next, the Payment microservice is

It is noteworthy that, in synchronous communication paradigms, such as through REST APIs
and RPCs, the requester microservice gets blocked until the request terminates in the remote
microservice and the response is received. It is natural to deduce that such blocking times can
grow arbitrarily when business transactions traverse several microservices. In the example above,
Cart would necessarily wait until Payment terminates the payment processing. Through the
asynchronous paradigm, though, once the checkout_cart event is generated, Cart microservice is
free to allocate computational resources (e.g., threads, CPUs, and memory) to serve other requests.
This usually favors higher efficiency in event processing [139].

It is noteworthy that messages exchanged asynchronously among microservices are usually
semantically classified as commands or events by practitioners [93]. The former indicates a call for
action for the receiver microservices, representing an intent from the producer microservice. For
example, checkout_cart and place_order messages represent commands. The latter represents a
fact that has taken place in the past, such as price_updated and product_deleted. In practice,
they do not alter the behavior of microservices or systems supporting microservices such as
Message-Oriented Middleware (MOM) or Database Management System (DBMS). In this
work, we use the term event to refer to both.
2.3

Current Solutions and Challenges

The state-of-the-practice in event-driven microservice architectures often employs traditional web
application frameworks such as Spring, .NET, and Node.js to implement microservice components
and rely on MOM such as message queues and log-processing systems to enable persistent asynchronous communication [115]. Application programmers can also design event-driven microservices
through Function-as-a-Service systems [120] such as AWS Lambda [9] and actor systems such as
Orleans [24]. However, these have not arisen as dominant practices in event-driven microservice
architectures [12, 92, 184].

Besides the apparent attractiveness of a decoupled design, developers often end up encountering
challenges in managing events independently of the technologies adopted, as we discuss below. As
events are generated asynchronously, that is, producers do not wait for consumers’ consumption,
developers must account for the anomalies that possibly may arise in event delivery, as described
by the microservice adopter Nubank [142]:
“you have late-arriving events, you can make a payment that we receive that should be credited
on Friday, we need to time travel back, re-play the events and figuring out the balance that
does not invalidate the invariants we have.”
Besides, it is often reported that developers necessitate making sure anomalies in the event order
do not lead to problems in their microservices, as explained by an Uber team [59]:
“If it consumes an event that is not in sequence, our processing logic identifies the version
mismatch and we retry the event a number of times.”
Duplicated events can also pose risks to internal microservice states, as noted by Netflix [172]
below:
“Consumers should have idempotent behavior to account for any potential infrastructure and
producer mishaps […, and] should not repeat compute heavy operations for already-processed
messages.”
Events may not even arrive or fail to be processed by microservices, which can lead to misleading
assumptions about the global state, as noted by a Wix developer [180]:
“[…] developers noticed that the inventory service database does not reflect the actual inventory levels of some products. Was the OrderCreated or PaymentCompleted event produced
correctly?”
Turning to our example scenario, if price_update events are processed in different orders, that
can lead to incorrect product prices. Besides, supposing that the product_removal is not delivered
to and processed by both Order and Stock, that leads the system to an incorrect state. In addition, if
place_order is delivered more than once, that can lead to overcharging customers. An abnormal
burst of events can also pose challenges to the system if consumers cannot cope with the arrival rate
of events. As new events are generated, they often incorporate some data contained in previously
consumed events, leading to arbitrary types, content, and growth in the sizes of the events as they
flow across different microservices, posing performance challenges to the system.
In this work, we delve into these and several other types of challenges that affect event management in microservice architectures.

Study Design

Although event-driven microservices are a popular architectural style in industry settings [110,
115, 154], existing studies mostly overlook the practical implications of adoption and the challenges
brought about by an asynchronous and event-based design for microservices. Thus, the goal of this
work is to identify the practices adopted and challenges faced by practitioners developing software
systems using event-driven microservice architectures.
To achieve this goal, our study focuses on answering two Research Questions (RQs), as follows:

is little known why practitioners adopt this architectural style. Also, the literature is scarce

5.3

Performance

Performance is systematically cited as an important factor for adopting microservice architectures
[114, 137, 154]. Developers seek to reap the benefits of having the application decomposed into
independently scalable building blocks [115]. In an architecture based on events, though, the
performance of individual microservices is tightly coupled with their capacity to process and
dispatch application-generated events at a rate that does not compromise the expected performance
of the downstream microservices in the event flow. As a result, disruptions in the event flow can
lead to critical performance impacts. In this section, we analyze such cases through the lens of
microservice developers.

consumption request.

be possible in case the filtering criteria cannot be extracted from the event payload (e.g., which
contacts contain an invoice). In this case, it would be necessary to correlate the event with some data
belonging to a specific microservice private state, creating challenges due to the state encapsulation
property.

prohibitive cost on performance.
Developers benefit from the application data that is often embedded in events to build a view of
the application state. We observe the incidence of leveraging events to build views in CAS6. The
difficulties expressed by developers, the ad-hoc approaches put forth, and the disdain for stream
processing engines suggest that streaming systems could evolve to provide a better programming
interface and integration to developers to better meet their expectations.

Threats to Validity

In this section, we discuss the threats to the validity of our empirical study.
Selection Bias of the Source. Similar to previous research [13, 213, 216], our work uses SO as
the data source to study the challenges developers encounter in practice. Although other data
sources could supplement the findings, the heterogeneity of challenges, application scenarios, and
developer expertise found in SO enables a trustworthy reflection of the state of the practice. For
instance, the findings are aligned with several blog posts that report similar challenges [32, 59, 81,
142, 143, 175, 176, 179, 180, 217].
Construction of Tag Set. We select a set of tags to filter SO questions associated with event
management in microservice architectures. The relevant keywords and metrics applied may omit
some tags associated with event management in microservices. To mitigate this threat, we rely on
manual inspection conducted by independent researchers, and we adopt the lowest threshold used
in previous work [13, 213, 216] to include the highest amount of tags as possible.
Subjectivity of researchers. Since we adopt manual analysis to identify and categorize challenges,
this may threaten our findings’ validity. To minimize this threat, the first two authors analyzed and
classified each filtered question in isolation. Upon conflicts, an experienced arbitrator (last author)
coordinated the discussion to reach an agreement.
Generalizability of Our Findings. We recognize that the application scenarios, requirements, design
choices, event processing patterns, and the challenges extracted from SO may not be generalizable
to all possible cloud and data platforms to which event-driven microservices can be deployed.
However, as SO is an industry-strength questions and answers platform for developers, their
questions tend to shed light on the most adopted industrial practices. Thus, the 10-year question
range covered in this study strengthens our perception that the findings highlight a substantial
portion of the challenges in the state of the practice.

Multitier programming [211] is a paradigm to develop the components that spans across different
tiers in the system (e.g., client, server, and database) in the same compilation unit. Multitier
programming targets enhancing maintainability and program comprehension, and enabling formal
reasoning about the properties of the distributed application as a whole [211]. They do so by
generating correct code at runtime or relying on the compiler through code-centric methods such
as user annotations, static analysis, and types. Despite the multitier characteristic of event-driven
microservice architectures (message broker as a tier interconnecting multiple microservices), we
have not found evidence of the use of multitier programming in this study. Industrial solutions
related to the adoption of multitier programming often relates to user-facing services [211].
Choreographic programming refers to the definition of a concurrent system through a single
compilation unit called choreography [134]. More specifically, a choreography defines a precise
specification of the communication behavior of a system [133]. In contrast to the traditional
approach of specifying the components and a separate set of APIs that defines the input and output
messages of each, a choreography prescribes the specification of the expected communications
among components in advance, forming a global description of the system. The specification is
used to generate a correct implementation of each component, e.g., deadlock-free interactions.
Similarly, synthesis of implementations from message sequence charts (MSCs) [49, 50] can
generate executable system components based on the properties specified in MSCs. MSCs are
a formal visual language to express the interactions between components, including processes,
messages, and ordering relations. MSCs appear to be particularly helpful in spotting errors early in
the design, such as possible data races [113]. A commonly mentioned drawback of visual tools that
model concurrency is diagram complexity, specially in large-scale and complex code bases, which
can affect maintainability and execution flow understanding [166].
In the space of general-purpose programming languages, extensions that account for loosecoupleness and event-driven features are interesting to support developers to adhere to microservices requirements. We note particularly extensible extension languages like Lua [95], which offer
varied language features that can be combined to build coordination abstractions for distributed
asynchronous computing, namely, closures, coroutines, dynamic execution environments, and functions as first-class values [181]. Programming languages’ features aside, general-purpose languages
often offload coordination correctness to application developers. In this realm, Argus [121] is known
for offering both a programming language and system to design and execute distributed programs.
Although not having a modern implementation, Argus has influenced several developments over
time, like middleware frameworks such as CORBA and Java RMI, object-oriented databases, and
distributed object-oriented frameworks like Orleans (discussed below in actor model).
Despite the advantages of choreographies and the synthesis of implementations from MCSs, we
have also not found evidence of the use of choreography languages like Chor [133] and related
technologies, or tools and frameworks that support the MCS synthesis in this study.
We believe that tools and languages for multitier programming, choreographic programming,
and MCSs synthesis could be leveraged to reason formally about some of the properties pursued by
practitioners studied in this work. However, additional studies could survey practitioners’ rationale
and requirements in order to better understand the incurred adoption friction. We conjecture that
the description of a global behavior of a system can be seen as dissonant to the decentralized, independent, highly dynamic nature of components and communication channels, and the decoupling
of components pursued by practitioners in event-driven microservice architectures.

with the expected types, and do not lead to deadlocks [94]. In this line, we highlight Effpi [173], a
toolkit that provides a set of facilities to program and execute strongly typed concurrent, distributed,

Coordination programming focuses on the interaction of the components from a higher-level

is stored as typed objects called tuples [29]. The tuple space is used for communication between
processes.
Tuple space has been implemented in many industry-strength programming languages and
runtimes like Java, .NET, Python, and Ruby. A popular Java implementation of tuple spaces is
JavaSpaces [126], which treats Java objects as the record unit of a distributed system state. JavaSpaces

Bach [100] build upon the Linda model and present embeddings in Scala [145]. We note that tuple
space has not arisen as a popular paradigm in modern distributed applications [184]. Despite the
decoupling aspect provided by tuple space, in which processes do not need to refer explicitly to
each other, the shared memory model is at odds with the principle of independent and encapsulated
states in microservice architectures.
The actor model is a model for concurrent computation in distributed systems [2]. Actors model a
small unit of computation with an encapsulated state and interact via message-passing semantics. To
send a message, an actor must specify the target. A mail system buffers the messages and eventually
delivers them. An actor accepts only a single message at a time and can perform local state changes,
send a message, and create other actors. Concurrency in an actor system is enabled via pipelining
and dynamic actor creation, being only constrained by the available hardware resources hand the
logical dependence among actors.
Actor systems are realized either via a native programming language (e.g., Erlang) or an application framework (e.g., Akka for Java programs and Orleans for C# programs) support. In both
cases, they posit their own communication mechanism, via actor message-passing, often realized
by asynchronous RPCs. Although microservices can be implemented with an actor language or
framework, by bypassing a MOM such as Kafka, the default communication mechanism of these
systems excludes microservices that are not implemented as part of the same actor system.
Microservice developers could, in principle, forward actor messages to the MOM. Despite creating
a duplicated message in the system, which creates the necessity of mapping external responses
to system messages, by opting for such an approach, developers inherit challenges discussed in
Section 5.1, such as CAS1, CAS2, CAS4, and CAS7. Similar reasoning applies to choreography,
message-passing, multitier, and domain-specific languages.
Choreographies, session types, actor systems, and programs derived from multitier programming

service, ensuring durability of messages even under failures, message delivery order, and providing
high availability via fault-tolerance mechanisms, parallelism, load balancing, and supporting the
dynamic creation and deprecation of message channels, producer and consumer roles. It is worth

described in this work apply to both cases.

7.2.3 Observability. In relation to observability challenges, we highlight some research work

note that dependent session types allow expressing properties about the data in the communicating
messages [195]. For example, specifying that a message type can only be sent after a specific value
has been received. This requirement can appear in many challenges uncovered in this work, like
CAS2, CAS3, CAS5, CAS6, and so on. On the other hand, compared to session types, dependent
session types appear to lag behind in terms of tooling maturity and support for industry-strength
languages.

on introducing runtime monitors in participants (i.e., components) to check the conformance of
each to the multiparty session types properties that are defined through a Python API, allowing for
dynamic verification of distributed processes.

verifying data-aware processes [80].

Concluding Remarks

In this article, we mine and analyze several relevant SO questions to characterize the state of the
practices and the challenges microservice developers face while managing events. We identify key
patterns developers use while attempting to realize their functional and NFRs, suggesting a tension
between achieving requirements related to data consistency, loose coupling, and performance.
To further understand these tensions, we manually examined 628 sampled questions and identified
key challenges microservice developers face in varied application scenarios. These include issues
related to queuing, delivering, and processing events at the application level, as well as monitoring
and securing the event flow. Based on the myriad of practical findings, we provide actionable
implications for messaging systems, framework maintainers, cloud providers, and researchers. The
insights obtained from our work can benefit different stakeholders involved in the development
and management of event-driven microservice architectures:
— For practitioners: The challenges reinforce the importance of reliable sources of information and
the need for careful analysis of the documentation of the technologies they adopt. Practitioners
should question gray literature with general and high-level instructions that often do not
embrace the complex nature of problems faced in production settings. Practitioners should not
overlook problem-prevention measures, which include running, testing, and deploying their
applications under the expected workload prior to production. Besides, developers must be
cautious about “reinventing-the-wheel” approaches, since for every possible design attempt,
there are reports of previous attempts.
— For researchers: Microservice developers, on opting for asynchronous designs, end up encountering a myriad of challenges that reveal a surprising number of unmet needs. The varied
categories of problems uncovered, together with the correctness properties looked for by
developers, highlight timely requirements and provide a reference for future research, which
should cut across and thus involve different research communities. In particular, the findings
evidenced in this work should encourage research communities to investigate better systems,
algorithms, tools, programming models, and improved support for this growing community
of application developers.
— For tool builders: Documentation must explicitly highlight and address the problems faced in
practice. They must also provide actions that can be taken to mitigate the shortcomings of the
existing tools. Furthermore, vendors must be clear about what requirements and guarantees
